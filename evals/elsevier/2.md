# Introduction
Cacao (Theobroma cacao L.) is a crop of significant economic importance worldwide, particularly in countries of major production, including Brazil, Ecuador, Côte d'Ivoire, and Ghana (Lahive et al., 2019). Globally, approximately six million smallholder farmers in Latin America, Africa, and Asia are responsible for most of the cacao production, playing a pivotal role in supplying the international market (Beg et al., 2017; Rice and Greenberg, 2000). In Peru, cacao bean production exhibited an average annual growth rate of 17.3 % from 2020 to 2024 (Cámara Peruana del Café y Cacao, 2025). In the initial six-month period of 2023, the country exported 46,700 tonnes of cacao and its derivatives, with a total value of US$162.8 million. This represents a 20.7 % increase in value and a 12.3 % increase in volume compared to the same period in 2022 (Ministerio de Desarrollo Agrario y Riego, 2023). This growth is primarily advantageous to small farmers, as the increase in global market prices can triple the profitability of cacao beans (Agencia Agraria de Noticias, 2024). The warm-humid climate of the Amazon region provides an optimal environment for cacao cultivation. Additionally, it is distinguished by its remarkable microbial biodiversity, which has been identified as a hotspot for microorganisms with high biotechnological potential (Stürmer and Siqueira, 2011). This microbial diversity influences the region's agricultural ecosystems positively, contributing to sustainable crop management, enhancing soil health and facilitating biocontrol processes that enhance cacao production (Guimarães et al., 2012).
However, the crop is adversely affected by several pests and diseases (Torres et al., 2019). One of the most important phytosanitary challenges is frosty pod rot. This disease has caused crop losses of up to 90 % (Correa et al., 2014; Phillips-Mora and Wilkinson, 2007). The fungus Moniliophthora roreri, native to Amazon region (Aime and Phillips-Mora, 2005; Díaz-Valderrama et al., 2022), induces deformation, necrosis, and internal rotting of fruits, forming white spots with high concentrations of infective spores in advanced stages of the disease (Torres-de la Cruz et al., 2020). Moreover, due to its high levels of virulence it is a destructive and difficult-to-control disease (Ramos-Calderón et al., 2022). Similarly, another highly important phytopathogen is Moniliophthora perniciosa, a fungus that infects cacao and, during its prolonged biotrophic stage, remains at low concentrations within the infected tissues (Teixeira et al., 2014). The management of M. roreri primarily relies on cultural practices. Other strategies include genetic control through disease-tolerant cacao accessions. For example, the CCN51 cultivar is a highly productive variety that can adapt to various climatic conditions and exhibits partial resistance to diseases (Kadow et al., 2013). However, given the persistence and aggressiveness of these phytopathogens, complementary strategies are still necessary.
Implementing good agricultural practices (GAP) is critical for ensuring the quality of crops and mitigating the impacts of diseases (Stubenrauch et al., 2018; Torres et al., 2019). GAP involves fundamental strategies to guarantee safety, health, and sustainability (Schulte et al., 2020). Despite this, challenges such as limited access to agricultural inputs and the spread of diseases and pests hinder efforts to increase productivity (Wessel and Quist-Wessel, 2015). In this regard, ensuring both the availability and effective use of these resources is essential for producing high-quality cacao beans and, consequently, achieving higher prices in international markets (Levai et al., 2015). Given that many Peruvian cacao farmers have adopted organic certifications as a strategy to improve their income, the implementation of GAP for cacao management is a need (Pokorny et al., 2021). A complementary strategy for disease management is the use of biological control agents (BCAs). Certain microorganisms can protect plants against pathogens, and the use of properly selected strains offers several benefits for agriculture (Boro et al., 2022). For example, the genus Trichoderma includes beneficial strains capable of selectively interacting with plants and the environment, although their performance depends on environmental conditions (Ayilara et al., 2023; Zin and Badaluddin, 2020). Trichoderma spp. are widely known for their antagonistic properties against various plant pathogens. They can parasitize hyphae, colonize the rhizosphere and produce hydrolytic enzymes such as chitinases and β-glucanases as mycoparasitic strategies (Chóez-Guaranda et al., 2023; Reyes et al., 2023). In addition, some Trichoderma strains also generate volatile compounds that inhibit pathogen development (Harman et al., 2004; Tyśkiewicz et al., 2022). Also, Trichoderma possesses the capacity to induce systemic resistance, promote root development and enhance soil health (Calvo and Zúñiga, 2010; Hernández-Lauzardo et al., 2007). Furthermore, it is effective in reducing cacao frosty pod rot, thus establishing itself as a sustainable strategy for the management of this disease (Seng et al., 2014). Native strains of Trichoderma spp. have intraspecific variability in their ability to parasitize, produce antibiotics, grow mycelia and sporulate against M. roreri (Reyes-Figueroa et al., 2016).
The use of bioformulations based on Trichoderma strains is a suitable and sustainable complementary strategy to conventional methods, significantly contributing to integrated management of agricultural diseases (Buojaylah et al., 2024). Among these, Trichoderma species, such as Trichoderma asperellum, T. viride, Trichoderma harzianum, Trichoderma virens and T. hamatum, have been extensively studied because of their capacity to regulate soil-borne fungal pathogens, thereby establishing themselves as pivotal instruments in the realm of biocontrol strategies (Harman et al., 2004). The shelf life of these bioformulations, which is mainly determined by their stability, depends to a large extent on adequate preservation and dehydration processes, as well as on the presence of protective compounds that preserve cellular integrity. However, their viability tends to progressively decrease during prolonged storage, which highlights the need to optimize formulation techniques to ensure their efficacy (Rojas-Sánchez et al., 2022; Mahde et al., 2019). Native Trichoderma strains, naturally adapted to local conditions can be effective biocontrol agents under field conditions (Villamil Carvajal et al., 2015). This suggests the potential of native strains as a complementary strategy for disease management. However, when used as active compounds of biofungicides, they must be properly tested.
The large-scale need for implementation of bio-formulated products still faces technological challenges (Chávez-Díaz et al., 2020). The formulation of biological fungicides as wettable powders facilitates their application in agricultural practices, ensuring the long-term viability of the biological agent and allowing for its uniform distribution across crops (Omara and El-maghraby, 2023). For bioformulation, the solid-state fermentation (SSF) has proven to be an effective and economical strategy for the large-scale production of Trichoderma spores. Moreover, the use of alternative substrates could reduce production costs without compromising efficiency (El-Tayeb et al., 2012).
This study evaluated the biocontrol potential of M. roreri through bioformulations based on native Trichoderma strains, with the aim of optimizing their application in cacao cultivation in the Amazon. For this purpose, strains selected for their high antagonistic capacity were incorporated as the active ingredient in bioformulations using two organic substrates with different costs and production efficiencies during solid-state fermentation. The quality of the bioproduct was assessed following the standards established by the National Agrarian Health Service of Peru (SENASA) (Gómez et al., 2013).

# Materials and Methods
2.1. Trichoderma strains and culture conditions
The strains Trichoderma afroharzianum CP24-6 (Leiva et al., 2020), Trichoderma BIF7-C3 (Accession Number PV915258), UCF18-M1 (PV915259) and UCPF4-C3 (PV915260) isolated from cacao rhizosphere soil in the Amazonas region of Peru, were selected. These strains are currently stored in the fungal collection of the Laboratorio de Investigación de Sanidad Vegetal (LABISANV) at the Universidad Nacional Toribio Rodriguez de Mendoza de Amazonas, Perú. For use, the strains were initially grown on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) at 28 °C for 15 days until the fungal mycelium covered the surface of the plate. To ensure culture purity, typical Trichoderma morphotypes were evaluated using a modified microculture method (Johnson, 1946). Additionally, the strains were examined using Laser Scanning Confocal Microscopy using a Plan Apochromat X20, 0.8-NA lens on a Zeiss LSM880 Microscope. To do so, Fungal cells were stained with SCRI Renaissance 2200 (SR 2200, Renaissance Chemicals, UK) (Harman et al., 2004), mounted in water and visualized after excitation by a 405-nm laser line collecting fluorescence between 415 and 480 nm. When required, the purified strains were refreshed under the aforementioned conditions.
2.2. Antagonistic activity
The antagonistic activity of Trichoderma spp. against the fully characterized pathogens M. roreri BG6 and M. perniciosa MEB39 (Accession Numbers PP267437, PP267497, PP267545) was evaluated through dual culture assays (Rahman et al., 2009). PDA plates (90 mm) supplemented with 0.05 μg/mL chloramphenicol were inoculated with a 5-mm mycelial disc of M. perniciosa MEB39 or a spore suspension of M. roreri BG6 (106 spores/mL). After 7 days of pathogen establishment, Trichoderma spp. (106 spores/mL) were inoculated on the opposite side of the plates and incubated at 28 °C. Control plates contained single inoculations of either the pathogen or Trichoderma. Antagonism was quantified at 7 and 15 days as percentage inhibition (PA) using the formula: PA (%) = [(RG − RGT)/RG] × 100 (San-Blas et al., 2012), where RG corresponds to the radial growth of the pathogen in control plates and RGT its growth under confrontation.
2.3. Preparation of fermentative bag and recovery of Trichoderma conidia
Rice and rice husk were employed as fermentative substrates following the methodology described in Fig. 1. The rice grains were purchased at the “Mercado Modelo de Chachapoyas” in the province of Chachapoyas, under the commercial brand “Casserita Extra,” distributed by Corporación Líder Perú S.A., while the rice husk was obtained from “Molino de Pilar Arroz - Plato Rico” in the province of Bagua. Rice husks were washed under running water for 10 min to remove sand and soil residues. Trichoderma spores were obtained from 7-day-old cultures previously incubated on PDA at 28 °C. Spores were collected by scraping and suspended in 5 ml of sterile distilled water. The spore concentration was adjusted to 106 spores/mL. The homogenized suspensions were inoculated into flasks containing 50 g of precooked rice, previously sterilized at 121 °C and 21 PSI for 15 min, and incubated at 28 °C under a 12/12-h light/darkness regime for 7 days. After incubation, 300 mL of sterile distilled water supplemented with chloramphenicol (0.05 μg/mL) was added to each flask, and 20 mL of the resuspended conidia were inoculated into fermentation bags containing either 400 g of parboiled rice or 200 g of rice husk supplemented with 0.05 % (w/v) molasses. The fermentation bags were made of polypropylene and had a puncture hole made using a sterile syringe. This perforation was sealed with surgical tape. Both rice and rice husk fermentative bags were incubated for 10 days at 28 °C under a 12/12-h light/darkness regime. Finally, conidia were recovered by washing the substrates twice with distilled water. The resulting conidia suspension was sedimented for 24 h, transferred to a plastic tray and dried in a forced-air oven at 35 °C for 48 h to obtain a dry spore powder.

2.4. Bioformulation
Approximately 100 g of dried conidia of each strain of Trichoderma spp. were used as the active ingredient. These were supplemented with 0.01 % v/v Tween 80, 250 g commercial corn starch soluble powder and 1 L of distilled water. The mixture was homogenized for 30 min manually and dried at 40 °C in a forced-air oven for 48 h. Finally, the bioformulation was bagged, sealed and stored at −4 °C until further use (Fig. 1, Step 10).
2.5. Quality parameters determination
Spores/g, pH, Colony Forming Units (CFU), purity, spore viability, and moisture were measured. Ten biological replicates were pooled into a single sample, from which six sub-samples were taken for analysis. For spore counting and pH determination, 1 g of the soluble bioformulation powder was dissolved in 100 mL of H2Od with 0.01 % Tween 80, followed by a 1:10 dilution. A 10 μL aliquot was transferred to a Neubauer chamber for spore counting (spore/mL). The pH was measured using a Thermo Scientific benchtop potentiometer (Orion Star™ A215) and the CFUs were determined using the serial dilution technique. Samples were plated in duplicate on PDA medium at dilutions of 10−5, 10−6 and 10−7, both with and without 0.05 μg/mL chloramphenicol, and incubated at 28 °C for 48 h. Purity (%) was determined by calculating the number of Trichoderma colonies relative to the total number of colonies observed, also taking into account the presence of contaminating microorganisms, such as other fungi or bacteria. Spore viability (%) was assessed using a 200 μL aliquot at a 10−4 dilution, plated on water agar medium, incubated for 18 h at 28 °C, and an area of 1 cm2 was observed under an optical microscope (ZEISS Primostar 3 iLED) with lactophenol blue staining to quantify the germinated spores. Finally, moisture (%) was evaluated by dehydrating 1 g of sample at 100 °C for 24 h; the moisture percentage (H) was calculated using formula H = ((MH–MS)/MH) x 100, where MH is the weight of the wet sample (g) and MS is the weight of the dry sample (g), following SENASA's recommendations based on the works of Gómez et al. (2013) and (Michel-Aceves et al., 2008).
2.6. Pathogenicity tests on CCN51 cacao
To evaluate the efficiency of bioformulations, in vivo pathogenicity tests were conducted on a 10-year-old farm of the CCN51 cacao cultivar, in the province of Bagua, department of Amazonas, Perú (5°44'02″ S, 78°25'00″ W). The experiment was established in a shaded agroforestry cacao system representative of smallholder practices in the Amazonian region, on moderately sloped land with uniform shade from canopy trees. The trials were conducted from October to November 2023, at the beginning of the rainy season, with daily mean temperatures ranging from 23.0 °C to 26.5 °C, and relative humidity remained consistently above 85 % according to Jaen station (National Meteorology and Hydrology Service of Peru, 2025). Using a randomized complete block design (RCBD), nine treatments were distributed across seven blocks, each consisting of three trees. Within each block, one pod was assigned per treatment, giving nine pods per block and seven biological replicates per treatment. Treatments were randomly allocated to pods within each block to minimize positional and tree-related bias. Selected pods were 2–3 months old and located at similar heights on the branches to ensure uniform physiological maturity and exposure. Eight treatments (T1 to T8) were established using the bioformulations prepared in Fig. 1. As described above, these treatments involved combinations of Trichoderma strains (CP24-6, BIF7-C3, UCF18-M1, and UCPF4-C3) with two different substrates (rice and rice husk). Additionally, a control treatment using H2Od was included (T9). The treatments were applied as a preventive measure against M. roreri, using 5 mL of a Trichoderma suspension at 106 spores/mL (Herrera et al., 2020). Each inoculated fruit was immediately covered with a sterile polyethylene bag as an external physical barrier. Bags were previously perforated with two holes at the bottom to prevent water accumulation. Twenty-four hours after the application of Trichoderma, a suspension of 106 spores/mL of M. roreri was sprayed onto the entire surface of the cacao fruits. The inoculated fruits were then covered with a sterile polyethylene bag and secured with a rubber band, following the protocols established by Phillips (1986). Cacao fruits inoculated with H2Od and M. roreri (in this order) were used as a control. Sixty days post inoculation, the incidence and severity of symptoms (both internal and external) were measured. External severity (ES) was evaluated using the “1 to 5” scale proposed by Sánchez López, 1982), where 1: Absence of symptoms, 2: Mild symptoms, 3: Moderate symptoms, 4: Severe symptoms, and 5: Fruits fully affected. The incidence (I) and severity of the symptoms (S) were calculated using the following formulas:
Incidence (I) (%) = ((IFS)/IFO) x 100, where IFS is the number of symptomatic fruits and IFO is the number of observed fruits (Sánchez López, 1982).
Severity (S) = (Σ (Severity scores of the affected fruits)/Number of affected fruits ∗ 5), Where Σ (Severity scores of affected fruits) is the sum of the severity scores assigned to each affected fruit based on a scale from 1 to 5, number of affected fruits refers to the total number of fruits showing symptoms of moniliasis, and 5 corresponds to the maximum severity score in the scale used, which is the modified formula of McKinney and y Davis (1923).
The experiment was conducted on cacao farms under controlled environmental conditions, with temperatures ranging from 18 to 27 °C. Moreover, to prevent external interference during the experiment, a barrier system comprising polyethylene bags was employed.
2.7. Statistical analysis
Appropriate statistical analyses were performed using the emmeans (version 0.11.0), ARTool (version 0.11.2), and multcompView (version 0.1.10) packages in RStudio (version 3.4.3). For the in vitro antagonisms assessment, a one-way ANOVA was conducted, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Quality parameters were compared using SENASA's recommendations as a reference. The in vivo assays was analyzed using parametric two-way ANOVA. When assumptions were met, the model was fitted using the aov function, with pairwise comparisons and treatment groupings determined using the multcomp::cld function with Sidak adjustment. Additionally, a Dunnet test was applied. A 95 % confidence level was used for all analyses, and differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Graphs were created using the ggplot2 package (version 3.5.2), and final adjustments to color scheme, typography, and label alignment were made in Illustrator (v 23.0.5), preserving the integrity of the original graphical data.

# Results
3.1. Strains morpho-cultural description
All Trichoderma strains displayed a fast mycelial growth rate, covering the entire surface of the plate within 5 days. Distinctive macroscopic characteristics typical of Trichoderma spp., such a cottony texture, concentric rings and white hyphae color were observed during the initial 3 days when grown on PDA medium. Over time, a dark olive-green coloration was observed on the colonies, resulting from the abundant production and accumulation of conidia on their surface. Confocal microscopy showed the distinctive features of hyphal structures, conidiophores, and conidia of the Trichoderma genus (Supplementary Fig. 1). While the conidiophores exhibited a typical tree-like structure of Trichoderma genus (S1A) the conidia –asexual spore– showed an ellipsoidal shape. Moreover a compact network of septated hyphae (S1D) was observed.
3.2. In vitro antagonistic capacity against M. roreri and M. perniciosa
The percentage of antagonism (PA) was evaluated at 7 and 15 d post-confrontation tests. Moreover, based on the growth patterns of Trichoderma and the pathogen, the type of interaction was distinguished as antibiosis when an inhibition zone was observed, and as mycoparasitism when physical interaction occurred, including the overgrowth of the antagonist on the pathogen. Although no significant differences were observed among the Trichoderma strains, the confrontation tests revealed that PA values increased over time, confirming a strong antagonistic capacity across all the Trichoderma strains against both M. roreri BG6 and M. perniciosa MEB39 (Fig. 2). When Trichoderma strains were co-cultivated with M. roreri, no significant differences were observed across them (Fig. 2A and B). However, notable antagonistic effects were particularly evident at 15 days post-confrontation for all Trichoderma strains, with PA values exceeding 99.5 %, with strain CP24-6 exhibiting the highest PA value against M. roreri BG6 (99.5 % ± 0.8) (Fig. 2B). In addition, we observed mycoparasitic activity of Trichoderma strains against M. roreri, in which Trichoderma hyphae surrounded and grew on the M. roreri mycelium (Fig. 2A). All Trichoderma strains exhibited similar antibiosis behavior against M. perniciosa (Fig. 2C and D). Strains CP24-6 and BIF7-C3 showed PA values of 36.8 ± 4.3 % and 39.3 ± 7.7 %, at 7 days post-confrontation. These values increased to 74.6 ± 3.3 % and 66.1 ± 4.2 %, respectively, at 15 d, reaching the highest antibiosis effects for M. perniciosa under in vitro conditions. In addition, strains UCF18-M1 and UCPF4-C3 evidenced PA values of 38.4 ± 5.1 and 38.6 ± 6.0 at 7 days, increasing to 72.6 ± 13.5 % and 71.3 ± 4.4 % at 15 d post confrontation. Moreover, unlike the M. roreri confrontation tests, mycoparasitic activity was not observed in the Trichoderma–M. perniciosa combinations. On the contrary, an inhibition zone (halo) was observed.

3.3. Bioformulation and quality parameter analysis
The quality parameters of Trichoderma solid-state fermentations using rice and rice-husk as substrates, were evaluated and compared to SENASA standards (Gómez et al., 2013). According to the Servicio Nacional de Sanidad Agraria of Peru, these standards include a minimum spore count of at least 109 spores/g, spore viability of at least 90 %, purity of at least 95 %, pH between 3.5 and 5.5, and a colony-forming unit (CFU) count equal to or greater than 109 CFU/mL, all of which are necessary to produce an effective product for disease management. Although the moisture is not considered by SENASA among the recommended quality parameters, it was also measured due to its close association with product stability and shelf life. This must be maintained below 10 %.
During the process described in Fig. 1, differential mycelial colonization was observed with parboiled rice showing a faster growth rate, becoming evident by the fourth day of incubation, compared to the rice-husk substrate supplemented with molasses. After this process, the quality parameters were measured (Table 1). Compared with SENASA, better quality performance was achieved when strains were grown using rice as the fermentation substrate. Under these conditions, all recommended parameters were met, except for the spore viability of strain UCF18-M1 (89.7 ± 2.4 %). In contrast, fermentations using rice husk resulted in spore viability falling below the recommended threshold; however, spore count, purity, pH, and CFU/g values did meet the established standards.

The results revealed higher spore counts (spores/g) and CFUs/g when the strains were cultivated on rice as a substrate. For spore production, strain CP24-6 yielded the highest number of spores (1.9 ± 0.2 × 1010 spores/g), compared to B1F7-C3 (1.3 ± 0.1 × 1010), UCF18-M1 (1.0 ± 0.1 × 1010), and UCPF4-C3 (1.2 ± 0.0 × 1010). In contrast, strains B1F7-C3 and UCPF4-C3 exhibited the highest CFU values (1.1 ± 0.1 × 1010 and 1.2 ± 0.6 × 1010, respectively), outperforming CP24-6 (6.5 ± 0.1 × 109) and UCF18-M1 (5.6 ± 1.0 × 109). Lower performance was observed for all strains when formulated on rice husk. Notably, strain UCF18-M1 showed the lowest spore concentration on rice husk (6.7 ± 0.9 × 109 spores/g). Regarding purity (%), germination (%), and pH, better performance was observed on rice compared with rice husk. In contrast, moisture content was higher when rice husk was used as a substrate.
3.4. Evaluation of the biocontrol efficacy of Trichoderma-based bioformulations in cacao fruits against M. roreri
Disease progression in the cacao fruit was monitored over a 60-day period after experiments were set up. Early symptoms included hydrosis and swelling, while advanced stages were characterized by the appearance of brown spots and fruit mummification with signs of the pathogen, such as white mycelium and stroma, and extensive fungal sporulation (Figura 3). At these advanced stages, the affected fruits displayed necrosis and tissue rot, with white, cotton-like mycelium covering the surface, followed by spore production—characteristic of frosty-pod rot caused by M. roreri (Fig. 3). The bioformulations proved effective in reducing both external (ES) and internal severity (IS) of M. roreri, with efficacy depending on the Trichoderma strain and the fermentation substrate used (Table 2, Fig. 4). A parametric two-way ANOVA revealed a significant interaction between strain and substrate for ES (p = 0.033). Multiple comparisons showed that all strains grown on rice as the fermentation substrate significantly reduced ES (28 %–40 % reduction) compared to those grown on rice husk (16 %–24 %). Notably, strain CP24-6 achieved the highest reduction in ES, reaching 48 % (Table 2), whereas UCF18-M1 and UCPF4-C3 grown on rice husk achieved only a 16 % reduction. Additionally, a Dunnett test comparing each treatment to the control confirmed a significant reduction in ES for all rice-based bioformulations, as well as for UCF18-M1 and UCPF4-C3 when grown on rice husk (Fig. 4). Among these, the most pronounced reductions were observed for CP24–6, B1F7-C3, and UCPF4-C3 formulated with rice (p < 0.05). For internal severity (IS), no significant interaction was detected between strain and substrate; however, a significant main effect of the strain was found (p = 0.0005). Similar to ES, the highest IS reductions occurred with rice-based bioformulations (28 %–56 %), whereas formulations based on rice husk achieved only 16 %–28 % reduction (Table 2). The Dunnett test also showed significant IS reduction for all rice-based treatments. Interestingly, in contrast to the ES results, CP24–6 and B1F7-C3 showed the lowest IS reductions among the rice-based treatments. Overall, the Dunnett test confirmed the superior efficacy of rice-based bioformulations in reducing both external and internal severity, with the most notable effects observed for strains CP24–6 and B1F7-C3 (Table 2 and Fig. 4). Disease incidence, measured by the ability to prevent infection completely, was also evaluated. Since all inoculated cacao fruits developed symptoms of M. roreri, disease incidence was 100 % across all treatments; therefore, no significant differences were observed among the bioformulations.

# Discussion
This study evaluated Trichoderma-based bioformulations as biocontrol strategies against M. roreri, focusing on the effect of different substrates for solid-state fermentation. Initially, selected native Trichoderma strains were tested under in vitro conditions to confirm their antagonistic capacity. Then, these strains were later used as active ingredients for the different bioformulations. All native Trichoderma strains demonstrated strong in vitro antagonism against M. roreri, exhibiting antibiosis levels between 92.9 % and 99.5 % after 15 days. At similar time and conditions over 66.1 % of inhibition was observed for M. perniciona. The antagonistic activity of Trichoderma is attributed to several biological mechanisms, including mycoparasitism, nutrient competition, and antibiosis. The latter is associated with the production of toxic metabolites and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that inhibit the development of pathogenic fungi (Verma et al., 2007; Steyaert et al., 2003; Contreras-Cornejo et al., 2016; Tchameni et al., 2020). In particular, against M. roreri, the inhibitory effect of Trichoderma-derived metabolites has been reported (Bailey et al., 2009). Fatty acids such as nonanoic acid (NA) can inhibit up to 75 % of spore germination in vitro, although they exert minimal effects on mycelial growth. This inhibitory action is suggested to result from NA uptake, which prevents the internal pH increase typically associated with spore germination (Aneja et al., 2006). Other Trichoderma-derived compounds associated with antifungal activity include gliotoxin, peptaibols, harzianolides, and 6-pentyl-α-pyrone (Zeilinger et al., 2016). Similarly, against M perniciosa, a recent chemical characterization identified several antifungal molecules such as widdrol, β-caryophyllene, tyrosol, butyl isobutyrate, sorbic acid, palmitic acid, palmitelaidic acid, linoleic acid, and oleic acid (Chóez-Guaranda et al., 2023). The biocontrol potential of Trichoderma-based bioformulations is closely linked to the expression of genes responsible for the synthesis of bioactive molecules. Although the mechanisms of action are not fully understood, 1,6-diphenylhexane-1,3,4,6-tetrone produced by T. asperellum CCTCC-RW0014 has shown a strong affinity for the Snt2 protein of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cucumerinum (FOC). This union decreases pathogenicity and increases the intracellular degradation of FOC (Saravanakumar et al., 2016). Other mechanisms of Trichoderma include the induction of systemic resistance responses in plants (Morato et al., 2023; Shoresh et al., 2010; Matrood et al., 2020).
Among the Trichoderma strains, the highest inhibition of M. roreri was evidenced by the T. afroharzianum CP24-6.This strain exhibited superior colonization and growth on fungal mycelium compared to other strains, likely due to the inherent capacity of members of the Harzianum clade to express strong antagonistic activity against M. roreri (Leiva et al., 2022).
During the Trichoderma-M. roreri confrontations, mycoparasitism was observed across all strains evaluated. Mycoparasitism is a key mechanism in Trichoderma species, enabling them to inhibit the development of pathogens effectively (Benítez et al., 2004; Guzmán-Guzmán et al., 2023). The process is based on the production of lytic enzymes and specific bioactive compounds that degrade the cell walls of the host fungus, thereby interrupting its growth and life cycle (Sharma et al., 2018; Tchameni et al., 2020). Although, the mycoparasitism was described for several strains of Trichoderma growing with pathogens, such as Fusarium sp., Phytophthora infestans, Erysiphe necator and Fusarium solani (Alfiky et al., 2024; Ben Amira et al., 2017; Matarese et al., 2012; Sawant et al., 2017), it is not a universally observed behavior. This is because each strain of Trichoderma can modify its mechanisms of action in response to the specific pathogen and the environmental conditions (Guevara-Viejó et al., 2024). In agreement with this, no mycoparasitic activity was observed between the evaluated strains and M. perniciosa. This suggests the widespread genetic plasticity of Trichoderma strains in the environment.
Given that the Trichoderma strains exhibited similar antagonistic capacities, all were used as active ingredients in the formulation of biofungicides through solid-state fermentation (SSF) method. SSF is an effective and economical strategy for large-scale production of Trichoderma spores and, according to previous studies, can allow the use of alternative substrates to reduce production costs (El-Tayeb et al., 2012). In this context, two different fermentative substrates were tested: conventional rice grains and rice husk, the latter as a low-cost alternative. However, contrary to expectations, conidia produced on precooked rice grains exhibited superior quality. After the bioformulation process, the quality parameters recommended by the Servicio Nacional de Sanidad Agraria of Peru for fungal bio-fungicides were used as a reference. This effect was particularly evident in strains CP24–6, B1C7-C3, and UCPF4-C3, which showed reduced spore viability when grown on rice husk as the fermentative substrate. The optimal formulation conditions using the rice substrate can be attributed to its nutritional composition, which includes 70 % starch, proteins and other essential nutrients (Chávez-García et al., 2008). These conditions likely promote fungal growth and development, allowing it to achieve the desired quality standards for the bioformulation. Optimal conditions are critical for the stability and performance of bioformulations, as it ensures their competitiveness under challenging environmental conditions (Naeimi et al., 2019). Among the key quality parameters, spore production and moisture content are particularly important for successful fungal colonization and persistence (Cavalcante et al., 2008).
Although previous studies showed that the inclusion of molasses in alternative substrates such as rice husk, can enhance growth and conidia production (Agamez Ramos et al., 2009; El-Tayeb et al., 2012; Naeimi et al., 2020; Sachdev et al., 2018), in our study the addition of molasses resulted suboptimal outcomes for conidial germination and purity (only for CP24-6).
Despite the differences among bioformulations, the spore/g, CFU/g, Purity and pH met the SENASA recommended conditions. Therefore, all bioformulation (both rice and rice husk substrate) were used for subsequent in vivo assays and the severity and incidence of M. roreri was evaluated. M. roreri initiates infection by germinating on the surface of cacao fruits and subsequently penetrating the internal tissues, where it causes necrosis and decay. External symptoms, including lesions and sporulation, only become visible once the fungus has sufficiently colonized the internal tissues (Phillips-Mora and Wilkinson, 2007). These symptoms typically appear by the third week post-inoculation and are characteristic of frosty pod rot. During infection, the fungus actively degrades the cells, induces internal tissue degradation causing fruit decay.
After evaluation, all the Trichoderma-based bioformulations demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing both internal and external severity when formulated with rice as a fermentative substrate. Among them, the strains CP24-6 and BIF7-C3 achieved the most consistent and highest percentage reductions. Moreover, this performance was influenced by the interaction between strain and substrate factors. In contrast, rice husk-based bioformulations also resulted in significant reductions in internal and external severity, however, these results were more variable and strongly dependent on the fungal strain. Although both substrate and strains are proven to influence the bioformulations performance, the crucial role of the substrate is doubtless. It modulates fungal sporulation, viability, and antagonistic activity in the field (Naeimi et al., 2020). As it was aforementioned the effectiveness of rice-based formulations is linked to their moisture retention and nutrient availability. This may lead, under specific moisture and nutrient conditions, optimal growth and sporulations of the fungus (Michel-Aceves et al., 2008; Agamez-Ramos et al., 2009). Low-cost substrates like rice husk can also be viable when supplemented with additives such as molasses (Barrera et al., 2019); however, similar to in vitro results, no significant improvement was observed in this study. Nonetheless, other additives may still offer potential improvements. The efficacy of Trichoderma as a biocontrol agent also depends on strain-specific traits. These strains may differ in their biological mechanisms, such as enzyme production, secondary metabolite profiles, and adaptability to environmental conditions. As a result, the performance of the bioformulation can vary depending on the specific characteristics of each strain.
Significant reduction of external and internal severity were observed as a result of the different Trichoderma-based bioformulations. The decrease in ES suggests that these formulations can limit the progression of the disease to advanced stages on the fruit surface. Meanwhile, the reduction in IS indicates their effectiveness in preventing early infection and internal colonization by the pathogen. This is especially important because IS is a crucial factor in assessing the damage caused by M. roreri in cacao fruits, with a direct impact on bean quality (Phillips-Mora et al., 2005). Particularly, external severity was evidenced by the superficial appearance of sporulation. Although sporulation was significantly reduced, especially in rice-based treatments, it could still occur (e.g., T6 and T7). Because sporulation could not be quantified, it is important to note that, as previously reported for M. roreri, sporulation is positively correlated with lesion size and disease severity (Phillips-Mora and Wilkinson, 2007). Therefore, the significant reductions of external severity (ES) not only limit the visible damage to fruits but also likely decrease the concentration of the inoculum and the risk of secondary infections under field conditions. This finding is particularly relevant for integrated disease management, where reducing inoculum sources is essential for disrupting the epidemic cycle.
As both external and internal severity are key indicators of biocontrol efficacy, these results not only confirm the antagonistic capacity but also suggest the biocontrol effect of bioformulations based on these native Trichoderma and their potential as a complementary strategy for managing frosty pod rot disease. This is particularly important since biocontrol success often relies on native strains adapted to local environments (Savazzini et al., 2009), selecting locally adapted isolates remains a promising strategy for field application (Páez-Martínez et al., 2024).
In addition, we found a 100 % incidence of disease was observed in all treatments. Although this could suggest that the application of Trichoderma-based bioformulations was unsuccessful in preventing initial infection, Sánchez and Garcés (2012) point out that biological control does not entirely eradicate phytopathogens but rather reduces their population to a manageable level. Despite this, evidence of a significant reduction in FPR incidence has been reported for several Trichoderma strains. For example, the application of a Trichoderma spray solution reduced M. roreri incidence by 11 % (Seng et al., 2014). Similarly, biopreparations of Trichoderma E29 resulted in a final moniliasis incidence of 2.52 % (Guevara-Viejó et al., 2024). Moreover, it should be mentioned that the reduction effect on incidence by CP24-6 had been previously reported (Leiva et al., 2020), although this strain was not evaluated as a bioformulation under SENASA quality parameters. In addition, evidence for the reduction of deformed pods, as well as the decrease in black pod incidence, has also been documented as a result of Trichoderma bioformulation applications (Crozier et al., 2015).
The biocontrol potential of Trichoderma-based bioformulation strains has been previously documented. When applied as a foliar bioformulation—either alone or as part of a microbial consortium—Trichoderma has demonstrated a significant reduction in disease progression (Kumar et al., 2011; Şesan et al., 2020; Jambhulkar et al., 2018). Against M. roreri, a pioneering study demonstrated that weekly applications of Trichoderma reduced disease incidence between 26 % and 41 % (Soberanis et al., 1999). Similarly, the application of several Trichoderma strains under field conditions reduced moniliasis by 14.6 %–24.9 % compared to optimized cultural control alone (Krauss et al., 2006). In another study, T. asperellum Tr-4 was shown to simultaneously reduce the progression of frosty pod rot (FPR) and black pod disease when applied to cacao trees (Krauss et al., 2006).
Although these encouraging results suggest that our Trichoderma-based bioformulation could significantly reduce the frosty-pod disease, some current experimental limitations must be addressed. This study did not include commercial chemical fungicides, therefore future studies should incorporate chemical controls to provide a more comprehensive and accurate evaluation of the bioformulations’ efficacy. Additionally, since the assays were conducted under semi-controlled conditions, field trials are essential to confirm their effectiveness under real agricultural environments. Finally, as repeated applications throughout the crop cycle have been shown to improve biocontrol persistence and performance (Leach et al., 2002), future studies should evaluate optimal application frequency and duration.
5. Conclusions
All the evaluated native Trichoderma spp. strains exhibited strong antagonistic capacity against M. roreri under vitro conditions. particularly by the strain T. afroharzianum CP24-6.
The formulation based on rice grains results in superior quality compared to those based on rice husk. Similarly, the biological properties of Trichoderma strains are also crucial, as evidenced by the performance of the T. afroharzianum CP24-6 and BIF7-C3 strains.
The bioformulation of strains CP24-6 and BIF7-C3 based on pre-cooked rice substrate significantly reduces the internal and external severity of M. roreri.
These Trichoderma-based biological formulations may be promising and economically viable complementary strategies for sustainable disease management in cacao plantations, however their efficacy must be validated through larger-scale, multi-season field trials and optimized application strategies to benefit smallholder producers in the Amazonas region of Peru.
