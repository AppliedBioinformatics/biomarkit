# Introduction
Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of all cases (1). Among the histological subtypes of NSCLC, adenocarcinoma, especially when detected as ground-glass nodules (GGNs) through low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening (2), presents a significant clinical challenge. Early detection and accurate preoperative assessment of GGNs are crucial for optimizing patient management and prognosis.
GGNs can be categorized into precursor lesions (PL, such as atypical adenomatous hyperplasia [AAH] and adenocarcinoma in situ [AIS]), minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA), and invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC) (3). Distinguishing between these categories is critical as it significantly impacts clinical management and surgical approaches. For instance, PL and MIA typically require limited resection, whereas IAC generally necessitates lobectomy (4). However, differentiating the pre-invasive categories from the invasive pulmonary adenocarcinomas preoperatively is challenging due to the overlapping computed tomography (CT) features. Traditional diagnostic methods heavily rely on pathological examination, which is invasive and time-consuming. Recent advancements of artificial intelligence (AI) in medical imaging have provided new avenues for preoperative assessment (5). Radiomics offers a non-invasive approach and shows more promise in sensitivity, specificity, and experimental feasibility for disease diagnosis, tumor staging, and patient prognosis 6, 7. Deep learning approaches offer the potential to automate more complex image analysis, detect subtle holistic imaging findings, and unify methodologies for image evaluation (8). Models including machine learning and artificial neural networks have been applied in lung cancer diagnosis 9, 10, 11, achieving excellent identification efficiency and accuracy in tests. Yet, these uni-modality-based tools suffer from problems of overfitting and weak interpretability 12, 13, 14. Additionally, traditional radiomics uniformly calculates features within the region of interest, thereby overlooking intratumoral regional variations. In contrast, subregion-based habitat imaging analysis integrates image data mining with underlying tumor microenvironmental heterogeneity. This approach constructs an image-microenvironment bridge for tumors, potentially unveiling intratumoral heterogeneity to reflect tumor growth, drug resistance, pathological grading, and prognosis 15, 16, 17. To overcome these limitations, our study adopts a multi-modal approach by integrating traditional radiomic features, deep learning features, and clinical risk factors. Moreover, we also introduced an approach of "habitat analysis", which assesses the tumor microenvironment and its interaction with the tumor itself, providing insights into tumor biology 18, 19.
This dual-center study aims to develop and validate the comprehensive model that integrates non-contrast and contrast CT features, clinical risk factors, and intratumoral habitat variables to enhance the invasiveness predictions of GGNs. This study contributes to the evolving paradigm of AI-driven, patient-specific management in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and aligns with the growing emphasis on precision oncology.

# Materials and Methods
Study Population
This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review boards of two centers: General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University and Changzheng Hospital, the Second Afffliated Hospital of the Naval Medical University (Approval No. KYLL-2025–0677, 2018SL049), and the requirement for written informed consent was waived. All procedures performed in this study were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with a pure ground-glass nodule (pGGN) or a mixed ground-glass nodule (mGGN) with a diameter of ≤30 mm on thin-section CT, (Ⅱ) GGNs not shrinking or disappearing during 3 months of follow-up, (Ⅲ) patients with 0.625–1.25 mm thin-sliced NECT and CECT images that were obtained within the 2 weeks leading up to the operation, (Ⅳ) GGNs with a definite pathological diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) incomplete pathological results, (Ⅱ) patients without preoperative paired NECT and CECT scans, (Ⅲ) patients with radiotherapy or chemotherapy, (IV) patients with lung metastases, (V) poor quality images with respiratory motion artifacts, and (VI) patients with cavities in the nodules. From December 2018 to September 2023, a total of 396 patients who met the criteria were included in development data set. From July 2019 to November 2021, a total of 120 patients who met the criteria were included in external data set. All nodules were resected and diagnosed pathologically according to the 2015 IASLC/ATS/ERS classification system. The flowchart in Figure 1 displays the details of the patient enrollment. All patients were divided into the pre-invasive (PL and MIA) and IAC groups.

Chest CT Acquisition
All patients underwent CT scanning with 64-slice CT scanner (Siemens, Forchheim, Germany) and a 512-slice CT scanner (GE Revolution, America). The whole lung scan was completed after one inhalation and holding breath. The scanning range was from the thoracic entrance to the bottom of the lung. The protocol parameters were as follows: tube voltage, 120 kVp; tube current, 200 mA; thickness, 8 mm; pitch, 1.375; FOV, 387 mm; and acquisition matrix, 512 × 512. After the non-enhanced scan, contrast media (300 mgI/mL) was injected through the cubital vein for an enhanced scan at a flow rate of 3.0 mL/s with a flow volume of 60–80 mL. After the contrast agent injection, 100 mL of normal saline was injected. The two scanning phases were scanned in the 30 s, 60 s, respectively. After the scanning, the data were reconstructed by using the standard algorithm with a layer thickness of 1.0 mm. Image observation conditions: the lung window (window width: 2000HU, window level: −500HU) and mediastinum window (window width: 400HU, window level: 45HU). The appropriate window adjustment was made when necessary.
Image Preprocessing and Nodule Segmentation
The workflow of our research is shown in Figure 2. To minimize the differences caused by varying image acquisition parameters and thereby enhance the reproducibility of the results, we performed interpolation on all images, resampling all voxels to a uniform size of 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm × 1.0 mm. This step is crucial for eliminating anisotropy in the image acquisition process.

The primary GGN lesion area was manually identified and labeled by a radiologist with 4 years of experience in chest CT diagnosis and confirmed by another pulmonary radiologist with 7 years of experience. The nodule segmentation was determined in every 2-D CT slice with lung window settings (window width, 2000HU; window level, −500 HU) and mediastinal window settings (width, 400 HU; window level, 45 HU) using the artificial intelligence research platform of Huiying Medical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd.
Unsupervised Habitat Subregion Segmentation
K-means clustering was applied independently to three imaging sequences to partition each ROI into subregions. The Calinski-Harabasz (CH) index was used to evaluate cluster numbers ranging from 2 to 10. For all three sequences, the optimal CH score was achieved at K = 2. Consequently, each ROI was segmented into two subregions, yielding six habitat-based radiomics feature sets (A1/A2, D1/D2, N1/N2) to assess lesion heterogeneity. ITH-radiomics models were subsequently constructed by integrating subregion features of each sequence (A1/A2, D1/D2, and N1/N2).

Radiomics Feature Extraction
Radiomics features were extracted from each volume of interest (VOI) and clustering subregion, encompassing four categories: first-order statistical features, shape features, texture features, and filtered features. These features were extracted using the PyRadiomics package, which is a widely used open-source tool for radiomic feature extraction. First-order statistical features quantify the distribution of voxel intensities within the image using common statistical metrics, including mean, variance, skewness, kurtosis, etc. Shape and size features reflect the geometric properties of the VOI, such as area, perimeter, volume, aspect ratio, and sphericity. Texture features are derived from gray-level run-length matrix (GLRLM) and gray-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) calculations, including metrics like contrast, correlation, energy, and entropy, to characterize the spatial relationships and heterogeneity of gray-level distributions within the VOI. Higher-order statistical features are obtained by applying filters (e.g., exponential, logarithmic, square, square root, and wavelet transforms) to the original image, followed by feature extraction using the aforementioned methods to capture additional information from the transformed images.

Deep Learning Feature Extraction
A ResNet50 pre-trained model (initialized with ImageNet weights) was employed to extract deep features. The input data was preprocessed and cropped to retain only the largest cross-sectional slice of the lesion for model input. The network was fine-tuned for 20 epochs, with the epoch exhibiting the lowest test loss (val_loss) selected for feature extraction. The output of the final ReLU activation layer was used as the deep feature representation for subsequent modeling.
Model Construction
All feature sets underwent feature selection processes before model construction, including variance thresholding (features with variance less than 0.8 were removed), univariate selection (features that were not significant were also eliminated), and least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression. The model for predicting the invasiveness of GGNs is constructed based on a multivariate logistic regression algorithm. The diagnostic classification models developed include radiomics models, deep learning model, ITH models, and the comprehensive model.
Sequence-based radiomics models were constructed. Specifically, subregion features were integrated for each sequence, which were then used to establish three ITH models. Optimal features from radiomics models (including both traditional radiomics and ITH radiomics) were selected and combined with deep learning features and key clinical variables to construct the comprehensive model.
Statistical Analysis
All statistical analyses were performed using Python (version 3.7.0; Python Software Foundation, USA) and R (version 4.1.0; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) with appropriate packages. Continuous data variables were expressed as the median and interquartile range (IQR), and categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. We analyzed between-group differences to reflect differences in patient demographic, clinical, and imaging characteristics. All parameters showing a statistical trend (P<0.05) in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate regression model to identify parameters independently associated with the invasiveness of GGNs. Independent risk factors of the invasiveness of GGNs with the forward selection procedure, retaining variables with P<0.05, were selected for multivariate regression analysis.
The area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to evaluate the discriminative ability of the proposed models. Calibration curves and Hosmer-Lemeshow tests were used to examine the model calibration. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to assess the value of the clinical application of the models and to compare the net benefits of the models.

# Results
Patient Characteristics and Data Grouping
A total of 516 patients with pathologically confirmed GGNs (PL = 135, MIA = 100, IAC = 281) were retrospectively enrolled. The cohort was divided into a training set (276 patients: 122 pre-invasive and 154 invasive), internal-validation set (120 patients: 53 pre-invasive and 67 invasive), and external-validation set (120 patients: 60 pre-invasive and 60 invasive). There were no significant differences in demographic characteristics between the pre-invasive (AAH/AIS/MIA) and invasive (IAC) groups. Among the clinical characteristics analyzed, nodule type and white blood cell (WBC) count demonstrated significant differences (P<0.05) between the two groups, whereas no statistically significant differences were observed in other variables (Table 1, Table 2).

Feature Extraction and Selection
A total of 1688 radiomics features were extracted from each VOI and clustering subregion. Additionally, 128 deep learning features were derived from a fine-tuned ResNet50 model. All models were constructed using scores calculated after feature selection. The scoring method involved summing the weighted features (the product of the Lasso algorithm-fitted feature coefficients and the feature values) for all patients. Since the ITH-A model demonstrated superior performance among all radiomics models (Table 4), the ITH-score from the ITH-A model, the Deep_score from the deep learning feature model, and key clinical factors were used as inputs for the comprehensive model. The optimal features and their corresponding coefficients involved in the ITH_score and Deep_score are shown in Table 3.

Model Performance
Eight models were constructed via logistic regression. Specifically, three radiomics-based models (RS-N, RS-A, RS-D) and three habitat subregion models (ITH-N, ITH-A, ITH-D) were developed based on non-contrast CT scans, arterial phase, and delayed phase images, respectively. In addition, one deep learning model (DL) was established, and a comprehensive model integrated habitat radiomics features (ITH-A), deep learning features, and clinical factors (nodule type and WBC count) to harness multi-modal information synergistically (Table 4).

The comprehensive model achieved the highest performance. Specifically, in the internal validation set, it yielded an AUC of 0.90 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.86–0.95), an accuracy of 82%, a sensitivity of 88%, and a specificity of 74% (Fig. 3a and 3b). In the external validation set, it demonstrated an AUC of 0.85 (95% CI: 0.80–0.91), along with an accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity all reaching 80% (Fig 3g).

In contrast, the intratumoral heterogeneity (ITH) models—built on tumor sub-regional heterogeneity features—achieved AUC ranges of 0.87–0.89 and 0.82–0.84 in the internal and external validation sets, respectively; these models exhibited high sensitivity but relatively lower specificity. The traditional radiomics (RS) models, which rely on conventional radiomic features, showed balanced performance across different scanning phases, with AUC ranges of 0.85–0.86 (internal validation set) and 0.81–0.83 (external validation set).
The deep learning (DL) model, which automatically extracts features via deep learning algorithms, achieved an AUC of 0.88 in the internal validation set and 0.83 in the external validation set. Overall, it outperformed the RS models and some ITH models but was slightly inferior to the comprehensive model (which integrates multi-modal features).
Although certain unimodal models (e.g., ITH-A, DL) approached the comprehensive model in specific metrics, the comprehensive model maintained significant advantages across the training set, internal validation set, and external validation set—with AUCs of 0.92, 0.90, and 0.85, respectively. These values were markedly higher than those of traditional radiomic models (RS series: AUC 0.86–0.88) and habitat models (ITH series: AUC 0.87–0.89). Additionally, the comprehensive model exhibited more balanced performance in terms of accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.
Furthermore, decision curve analysis confirmed that the comprehensive model provided the highest net benefit when the threshold probability was ≥0.4. This finding indicates that integrating multi-modal features effectively enhances the predictive efficacy for ground-glass nodule (GGN) invasiveness (Fig 3c and 3d).
Dynamic Nomogram
To facilitate clinical application, a nomogram combining the deep score, ITH score, nodule type, and WBC count was created for predicting the invasiveness of GGNs (Fig 4). In the nomogram, the score includes the single item score (points in the figure), which represents the single item score corresponding to each variable, and the total points represent combined with the single item score corresponding to all variables. The total score for GGNs corresponds to the probability of invasive. DCA analysis showed that the nomogram provided the highest net benefit when the threshold probability was ≥0.4, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed good calibration (P > 0.05) (Fig 3c-f,h–i). Additionally, we upgraded the nomogram to a dynamic nomogram and uploaded it to the shinyapps.io platform (https://younjing.shinyapps.io/Dynamic_nomogram_250402/) to predict the invasiveness of GGNs (Fig 5).

Explainable Analysis
We conducted interpretability analysis on the model using SHAP interpretability analysis. From the SHAP bar chart (Fig 6a), it can be seen that three parameters, Deep_score (Shapley = 0.82), Nodule_type (Shapley = 0.75), and ITH_score (Shapley = 0.7), made greater contributions to the model's prediction. The SHAP beeswarm chart (Fig 6b) shows that Deep_score and ITH_score made positive contributions to the model's prediction, while nodule type and WBC count made negative contributions to the model's prediction.

Additionally, we conducted an analysis of the prediction processes for 4 patients correctly diagnosed by the comprehensive model (Fig 7), systematically investigating the patients' demographic data, visualized results of lesion cluster analysis, numerical values of key sub-regional features, and the final input values of Deep_score and ITH_score. The results showed significant differences in key features and score values between patients with invasive and pre-invasive lesions, a finding that strongly supports the interpretability and reliability of the model's diagnosis.

# Discussion
The accurate preoperative differentiation of pre-invasive and invasive pulmonary adenocarcinomas in GGNs remains a critical challenge, as overtreatment of indolent lesions and undertreatment of aggressive tumors both carry significant morbidity. This dual-center study is the first to integrate radiomics features, deep learning features, and ITH features of CT images, and clinical variables into a comprehensive model for predicting GGNs invasiveness, providing a novel tool for clinical decision-making in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma. We addresses this gap by developing a dynamic nomogram to predict GGNs invasiveness. This model demonstrates robust performance (AUC: 0.92 in training set, 0.90 in internal-validation set, 0.85 in external-validation set) across multiple sets, offering a non-invasive tool to guide personalized surgical and management strategies.
In this research, we highlight two salient clinical factors, including nodule type and WBC count, contributing to the differential invasiveness of GGNs. Observing solid density in GGNs indicates that the lesion has already exhibited invasive elements, serving as a high-risk factor for invasive lesions, consistent with the radiologists’ experience. Previous researches 20, 21 have used nodule size and consolidation-to-tumor ratio to distinguish pre-invasive from invasive lesion. We observed that an increase in total WBC count was associated with a greater risk of invasive adenocarcinoma. This observation is in agreement with previous epidemiologic studies of WBC count and lung cancer development 22, 23, 24, 25. Inflammatory cells and factors in the tumor-promoting inflammatory microenvironment facilitate lung cancer development by inducing immune evasion, angiogenesis, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, apoptosis, and other related mechanisms (26).
In this study, we integrated various feature types based on multi-modal CT scans to predict IAC in GGNs. The final features included texture, high-order, and deep learning features from arterial-phase CT subregions and deep learning models. Texture features like wavelet-based GLCM and GLSZM captured tumor heterogeneity and microenvironment complexity, linked to tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and immune cell infiltration. Higher values indicated complex cell structures and environments, aligning with IAC's invasive nature. High-order features, derived from gradient and logarithmic transformations, detected subtle grayscale changes and patterns, revealing tumor-specific perfusion and density. Deep learning features, extracted by deep neural networks, captured complex patterns beyond human perception, related to advanced tumor biology, enhancing the model's predictive power. The combination of these features reflected tumor biology comprehensively and improved IAC prediction accuracy. Adding arterial-phase CT subregion features provided extra microenvironment information, potentially aiding in distinguishing IAC from pre-invasive lesions.
The deep learning model is a neural network model with multiple levels of data representation. As a new analytic tool, the deep learning network model will likely become more widely applied to support clinical decision-making (27). The deep learning approaches can be grouped into unsupervised, reinforcement, and supervised learning. A deep learning-based CAD system has been reported as a promising tool for the automatic diagnosis of lung disease in medical imaging with significant accuracy 28, 29. Sahu et al. (30) proposed a lightweight multi-section CNN with a classification accuracy of 93.18% for the LIDC-IDRI data set to improve accuracy. Jing Gong et al. (14) developed a deep learning-based artificial intelligence (AI) scheme for predicting the likelihood of the GGNs detected on CT images being invasive adenocarcinoma with accuracy of 92%. In our study, the deep learning model has similar results with an accuracy of 90% for predicting invasiveness of GGNs in train cohort and 88% in internal-validation cohort.
The nomogram demonstrated high discriminatory power, with AUCs of 0.90–0.92 in the training/internal-validation sets and 0.85 in the external-validation set for distinguishing IAC from pre-invasive lesions (AAH/AIS/MIA), underscoring its clinical utility. A critical innovation lies in the incorporation of habitat analysis, which partitions tumors into subregions (a1/a2, d1/d2, n1/n2 via K-means clustering) to capture spatial heterogeneity reflecting tumor biology. This approach slightly outperformed traditional whole-tumor radiomics, aligning with prior studies demonstrating the value of spatially resolved biomarkers. Habitats are believed to represent tumor evolutionary dynamics, whereby tumor cells are influenced by their growth environment (e.g, local blood flow, interstitial edema, and cell density), clustering into spatially heterogeneous subregions. Moreover, different habitats in tumors may exhibit unique patterns of growth and invasion, may respond differently to treatments, and may show regional heterogeneity in tumor genotypes and phenotypes 31, 32. Shang et al. (33) developed and validated a habitat model combining tumor and peritumoral radiomic features on chest CT for predicting invasiveness of lung adenocarcinoma with an AUC of 0.932 in the test set.
Compared to existing guidelines (e.g., Fleischner Society recommendations), the nomogram provides a dynamic, quantitative tool that reduces reliance on subjective morphological assessment. Its high specificity (84% in train set) could spare patients from unnecessary invasive procedures while ensuring aggressive tumors are appropriately treated. The dynamic nomogram (accessible at https://younjing.shinyapps.io/Dynamic_Nomogram_250402/) enables real-time clinical decision support, a critical step toward precision oncology. Notably, the nomogram outperformed conventional radiomics models (AUC: 0.85–0.88 in internal-validation set), underscoring the added value of multi-modal data in cases where morphological overlap between pre-invasive and invasive lesions complicates diagnosis. Given the methodological constraints inherent in multi-center prospective validation of predictive models, particularly regarding standardization of CT acquisition protocols and longitudinal clinical data harmonization, we employed DCA to evaluate the clinical applicability of nomogram. The analytical framework demonstrated superior net benefit when the clinical utility threshold exceeded 0.4, outperforming both universal intervention and non-intervention strategies across this probability spectrum. These findings collectively suggest that the comprehensive model holds significant potential for optimizing therapeutic decision-making processes and advancing personalized treatment strategies in GGNs management.
Additionally, we conducted an analysis of the prediction processes for 4 patients correctly diagnosed by the comprehensive model, systematically investigating the patients' demographic data, visualized results of lesion cluster analysis, numerical values of key sub-regional features, and the final input values of Deep_score and ITH_score. The results showed significant differences in key features and score values between invasive and pre-invasive patients, a finding that strongly supports the interpretability and reliability of the model's diagnosis. This case-level interpretive analysis further highlights the model’s ability to capture biologically relevant heterogeneity: for instance, invasive lesions exhibited distinct sub-regional texture gradients and higher Deep_score values, potentially reflecting tumor microenvironmental complexity or angiogenic activity. Such insights not only enhance confidence in the nomogram’s clinical utility but also lay a foundation for mechanistic exploration of imaging-pathology correlations, addressing a critical gap in current radiomics research.
Although we tried to make this study more complete and meaningful, there are still some limitations. First, the sample size is small, and subsequent efforts should focus on increasing the number of cases and involving more research centers. Second, the absence of longitudinal follow-up limited the assessment of long-term recurrence rates. Therefore, we plan to conduct future follow-ups to monitor the patients' postoperative outcomes. Finally, the preoperative prediction model we developed was primarily designed to compare its performance with that of clinicians. However, how to establish the specific application threshold (operating point) for this model in clinical practice remains undetermined and is a key focus for future research. This process may require a combination of cost and outcome analyses to carefully balance sensitivity and specificity.
CONCLUSIONS
This study proposed a comprehensive model for predicting GGNs invasiveness by integrating deep learning features, intratumoral habitat features, and clinic indicators (nodule type and WBC count), and further verified its potential in serving as a robust and interpretable tool in clinical practice. By bridging imaging and pathology, this model advances personalized surgical decision-making and aligns with Radiology’s mission to translate imaging innovation into patient-centered care.
