# Introduction
Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy in women, with rising global incidence rates and maintaining its position as the primary cause of cancer-related female mortality [1]. The implementation of early screening programs and accurate diagnostic approaches enables timely therapeutic interventions that significantly improve survival outcomes and quality of life in affected patients.

The tumor microenvironment (TME) constitutes a highly structured ecosystem comprising diverse immune cells, soluble mediators, and cellular receptors [2]. Mounting evidence highlights the TME’s pivotal role in orchestrating tumor progression [3]. Particularly, programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) has attracted increasing attention as a crucial immunoregulatory component of the TME [4]. Through its binding to programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), PD-L1 initiates co-suppressive signaling cascades that establish an immunosuppressive microenvironment, effectively enabling tumor cells to evade immune surveillance [5]. These findings are also evident in breast cancer, highlighting the pivotal role of PD-L1 in its progression and diagnostic potential as a biomarker for breast cancer diagnosis.

Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissue remains the gold standard for PD-L1 assessment [6], yet this approach suffers from intrinsic limitations including spatial heterogeneity, dynamic expression patterns, and the invasiveness of repeated biopsies, which collectively restrict its clinical utility as a reliable biomarker [7]. In contrast, circulating soluble PD-L1 (sPD-L1) has emerged as a promising alternative that overcomes these limitations by providing a systemic representation of tumor heterogeneity through non-invasive sampling. Substantial evidence demonstrates that sPD-L1 levels correlate with disease progression across multiple malignancies including lung, hepatocellular, and gastric carcinomas [8]. The current clinical biomarkers for breast cancer, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 15–3 (CA15-3), and cancer antigen 125 (CA125), exhibit suboptimal sensitivity and specificity with unsatisfied correlation to disease status [9]. Given its biological relevance and technical advantages, sPD-L1 represents a next-generation biomarker candidate capable of addressing these diagnostic shortcomings.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of circulating sPD-L1 levels and their correlation with systemic inflammatory indicators across benign and malignant breast disease cases and healthy controls from multiple perspectives. We also performed comparative assessments of the diagnostic accuracy between sPD-L1 and conventional biomarkers. Furthermore, we analyzed sPD-L1 expression in breast cancer cell line ultrastructures and elucidated its correlation with interleukin-6 (IL-6), a central immunoregulatory cytokine in the TME. Our objective is to identify a more precise and clinically valuable biomarker capable of improving early detection and reliably reflecting disease progression in breast cancer.

# Materials and Methods
Participants
Peripheral blood samples were obtained from participants between Jan 2022 and Dec 2023 in the Affiliated Taizhou People’s Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, with corresponding basic information extracted from electronic medical records. The breast cancer cohort comprised female patients (age ≥ 18 years) with histologically confirmed primary breast adenocarcinoma, excluding those with: (1) secondary malignancies, or (2) severe comorbidities (life expectancy < 5 years). Age-matched (≥ 18 years) female controls included: (a) healthy volunteers, and (b) benign breast disease patients, with exclusion criteria comprised: (1) malignancy history in the past 5 years, (2) acute or chronic infections (e.g., viral hepatitis, HIV), (3) systemic autoimmune diseases, or (4) other conditions causing chronic systemic inflammation (e.g., severe asthma, inflammatory bowel disease). All participants were screened via medical history reviews and laboratory testing to minimize confounding bias. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Taizhou People’s Hospital under protocol KY2022-146–01.

Detection of sPD-L1 and other parameters
In our study, sPD-L1 was measured using an ELISA kit (E-EL-H1547, Elabscience, China) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, with all samples analyzed in duplicate. Systemic immune features were evaluated via routine blood examination using the XN-20 Automated Haematology Analyzer (Sysmex, Japan). CEA, CA15-3 and CA125 were determined using the ARCHITECT i2000SR Immunoassay Analyzer (Abbott, USA). Additionally, IL-6 was detected using the UPT2800 Up-Converting Phosphor Immunoassay Analyzer (Hotgen, China).

Isolation of human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)
Human PBMCs were isolated using human lymphocyte separation medium (Haoyang Biological, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, fresh anticoagulated whole blood was diluted 1:1 with PBS and carefully layered onto the separation medium, maintaining a clear interface between the two solutions. After centrifugation at 800 g for 20–30 min, four distinct layers formed from top to bottom: diluted plasma, PBMCs, separation medium, and erythrocytes. The PBMCs layer was carefully aspirated and transferred to a new centrifuge tube, washed with 10 mL of PBS, and centrifugated at 250 g for 10 min, following washing with PBS for 2 times to obtain PBMCs.

Cell culture
MCF-10A cells were cultured in a specialized medium for breast epithelial cells (ZQ-1311, ZQXZBIO, China). MCF-7, ZR-75–1, SK-BR-3, and MDA-MB-231 cells were respectively maintained in MEM, RPMI-1640, McCoy’s 5A, and L-15 complete medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. The medium for MCF-7 was further supplemented with 10 μg/mL insulin. For subculturing, cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin–EDTA solution, collected by centrifugation, and resuspended in fresh mediums. For co-culture experiments, MCF-10A were co-incubated with MCF-7/ZR-75–1/SK-BR-3/MDA-MB-231 cells in the 12-well plate for 24 h using the specialized medium for breast epithelial cells at a 1:1 ratio of 2 × 105 cells/mL per well. Similarly, PBMCs were co-incubated with MCF-10A or MCF-7/ZR-75–1/SK-BR-3/MDA-MB-231 cells in the 12-well plate with a 1:1 ratio of 2 × 105 cells/mL per well, using RPMI-1640 medium with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin, for 24 h. Control groups consisted of each cell type cultured individually under identical conditions (2 × 105 cells/mL in 12-well plates). All cells were incubated at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. All unspecified cell lines and cell culture reagents were purchased from Shanghai Whelab Bioscience Co., Ltd.

Cell transfection and culture assays
PD-L1 siRNA (sense strand 5′-CUGAGAAUCAACACAACAAdTdT-3′, antisense strand 5′-UUGUUGUGUUGAUUCUCAGdTdT-3′) and primers (forward 5'-TGTACCGCTGCATGATCAG-3', reverse 5'-AGTTCATGTTCAGAGGTGACTG-3') were designed and synthesized by GenScript (China). Transfection reagents including Lipofectamine 3000 and Opti-MEM were purchased from Invitrogen (USA). Reagents for RT-qPCR were obtained from TakaRa (Japan). Western blot reagents were sourced from Proteintech (China). The CCK-8 assay kit was acquired from NCM Biotech (China).

MDA-MB-231 cells in logarithmic growth phase were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL per well. After cell attachment, complete medium was replaced with Opti-MEM following PBS washing. Cells were divided into three experimental groups: small interfering RNA (siRNA) PD-L1, siRNA negative control (NC), and blank control. siRNA PD-L1/siRNA NC and Lipofectamine 3000 were separately diluted in Opti-MEM, gently mixed, and incubated at room temperature to form transfection complexes. The transfection mixture was added to the wells and cells were cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 6 h before medium replacement, followed by additional 36-h culture before efficiency assessment. Transfection efficiency was evaluated using RT-qPCR and western blot. PD-L1 mRNA expression was detected by RT-qPCR, while PD-L1 protein expression was determined by western blot, with procedures performed according to manufacturer’s protocols.

The effect of PD-L1 knockdown on cell proliferation was assessed using CCK-8 assay. MDA-MB-231 cells in logarithmic phase were routinely transfected and seeded in 96-well plates at 2 × 104 cells/100 μL per well. Cell proliferation rate (%) was calculated as: [OD(experimental group)—OD(blank group)]/[OD(control group)—OD(blank group)] × 100%. Cell migration ability following PD-L1 knockdown was examined by wound-healing assay. Transfected MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 5 × 105 cells/2 mL per well. After cell attachment, scratch wounds were created on the monolayer, and cells were cultured in medium containing 1% serum for 24 h. Wound closure rate was observed and calculated under microscopy.

Statistical analysis
Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were employed to assess and compare the diagnostic accuracy of sPD-L1, CEA, CA15-3, and CA125. The optimal cut-off value for sPD-L1 was determined by identifying the point on the ROC curve that maximized Youden’s index (J = sensitivity + specificity—1). The cut-off values for CEA, CA15-3 and CA125 were adopted from pre-defined clinical thresholds. To evaluate the diagnostic performance of the combined biomarkers, we employed binary logistic regression analysis with breast cancer status (0 = negative, 1 = positive) as the dependent variable and serum levels of sPD-L1, CEA, and CA15-3 as continuous covariates, generating predicted probabilities for the multimarker panel and subsequently plotting the corresponding ROC curve to assess its discriminative ability. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p-values < 0.05. We employed multivariate logistic regression models to assess the independent predictive value of sPD-L1. We established a model with BC status (case/control) as the dependent variable, incorporating sPD-L1 while adjusting for age and inflammatory markers. We constructed a second model among BC patients utilizing staging status (early [I–II] vs. advanced [III–IV]) as the dependent variable. This model incorporated sPD-L1 while adjusting for key clinical-pathological parameters, including age, lymph node status, Ki-67 index, and case-type classification. We reported adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for all analyses and set the significance level at 0.05. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10.1.1 (GraphPad Software, USA) and SPSS Statistics 27.0.1 (IBM, USA). Between-group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney test. Correlational analyses between sPD-L1 levels and other parameters were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation.

# Results
Patient characteristics
The study enrolled 82 female breast cancer patients with clinical characteristics detailed in Table 1. The cohort had a median age of 55 years (range: 36–77), with invasive ductal carcinoma representing the predominant histological subtype (75.6%, 62/82). Lymph node involvement was absent in 41.5% (34/82) of cases, while 89% (73/82) showed no evidence of distant metastases. A subset of patients (13.4%, 11/82) had received prior treatments including surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy before study enrollment. Based on WHO staging criteria [10], 53.6% (44/82) were classified as early-stage (I-II) and 29.3% (24/82) as advanced-stage (III-IV) disease. Assessment of tumor proliferation revealed 48.8% (40/82) of patients exhibited high Ki-67 expression (≥ 30%), a well-established marker of aggressive tumor biology [11].

Circulating sPD-L1 expression across study populations
Serum sPD-L1 concentrations were quantified in 56 healthy donors (HD), 83 benign breast disease (BBD) patients, and 82 breast cancer (BC) patients, with BBD patient characteristics in Table S1. BC patients demonstrated significantly elevated median sPD-L1 levels (1.850 ng/mL, range: 0.098–8.643) compared to both HD (0.581 ng/mL, range: 0–2.114, p < 0.001) and BBD groups (0.727 ng/mL, range: 0–5.191, p < 0.001, Fig. 1A). This phenomenon also occurred in treatment-naive, non-metastatic, newly diagnosed primary BC cases (see Figure S1 for further discussion). Additionally, advanced-stage BC patients (stage III-IV) showed higher median levels (2.737 ng/mL, range: 0.098–8.643) than early-stage cases (1.322 ng/mL, range: 0.112–7.342, p = 0.006; Fig. 1B). Notably, distant metastases were associated with markedly increased sPD-L1 concentrations (median: 4.135 vs 1.613 ng/mL, p = 0.004, Fig. 1C), while lymph node metastasis showed a non-significant increasing trend (median: 2.390 vs 1.216 ng/mL, p = 0.078, Fig. 1D). No significant association was observed between sPD-L1 levels and Ki-67 proliferation index (median: 1.114 vs 2.201, p = 0.088, Fig. 1E). These findings positioned serum sPD-L1 as a promising biomarker for breast cancer, with particular relevance for advanced disease and metastatic potential.

Correlation of circulating sPD-L1 status with systemic immune parameters
We analyzed the relationship between serum sPD-L1 levels and systemic immune parameters in 221 samples, including complete blood count indices and derived ratios. Significant positive correlations were identified between sPD-L1 and both neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR, r = 0.237, 95% CI = 0.104–0.361, p < 0.001, Fig. 2G) and platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR, r = 0.202, 95% CI = 0.068–0.329, p = 0.003, Fig. 2H). Conversely, inverse correlations were observed with absolute lymphocyte count (r = − 0.209, 95% CI = − 0.335 to − 0.075, p = 0.002, Fig. 2D) and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR, r = − 0.281, 95% CI = − 0.401 to − 0.151, p < 0.001, Fig. 2F). No significant correlations were detected for white blood cell count (WBC, p = 0.554, Fig. 2A), absolute monocyte count (p = 0.129, Fig. 2B), absolute neutrophil count (p = 0.108, Fig. 2C), and absolute platelet count (p = 0.871, Fig. 2E).

Comparative analysis of sPD-L1 and conventional breast cancer biomarkers
In this comprehensive evaluation of 221 samples comparing sPD-L1 with conventional biomarkers (CEA, CA15-3, and CA125), we observed distinct diagnostic profiles (Fig. 3). CEA and CA15-3 levels were significantly elevated in BC patients (median: 2.235 ng/mL and 13.900 U/mL, respectively) compared to both BBD (1.400 ng/mL, p < 0.001; 8.400 U/mL, p < 0.001) and HD (1.095 ng/mL, p < 0.001; 7.545 U/mL, p < 0.001), whereas CA125 showed no significant differences across groups (Fig. 3A–C). Notably, none of the traditional biomarkers differentiated early- from advanced-stage BC (p = 0.972 for CEA, p = 0.439 for CA15-3, p = 0.248 for CA125, Fig. 3D–F). Further analysis revealed specific metastatic associations: CEA and CA125 were elevated in lymph node metastasis (median: 2.940 vs 1.565 ng/mL, p = 0.016, Fig. S2B; 13.600 versus 10.500 U/mL, p = 0.026, Fig. S2H), while CA15-3 showed marked increase in distant metastasis (median: 14.950 vs 13.850 U/mL, p = 0.025, Fig. S2D). None of the biomarkers showed significant associations with Ki-67 proliferation status (Fig. S2C, S2F, and S2I). While CEA and CA15-3 effectively discriminated BC from BBD/HD controls, they failed to differentiate early versus advanced disease stages. Correlation analysis demonstrated sPD-L1’s modest association with CEA (r = 0.260, 95% CI = 0.127–0.383, p < 0.001) and CA15-3 (r = 0.262, 95% CI = 0.127–0.387, p < 0.001), and weaker correlations with CA125 (p = 0.041) (Fig. 3G–I). These findings demonstrated that sPD-L1 outperformed conventional biomarkers in disease staging, highlighting its enhanced potential for breast cancer diagnosis and monitoring.

ROC curves and cut-off values of sPD-L1 and traditional biomarkers
ROC curve analysis revealed a high diagnostic performance of serum sPD-L1 for breast cancer, yielding an optimal cut-off value of 1.144 ng/mL (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.756, 95% CI = 0.690–0.823, p < 0.001), classifying 62.2% (51/82) of BC patients into the high sPD-L1 group (≥ 1.144 ng/mL) (Fig. 4A). For effective diagnostic discrimination, the rule-in and rule-out thresholds were respectively set at the concentrations corresponding to a specificity of ≥ 90% (2.115 ng/mL) and a sensitivity of ≥ 95% (0.170 ng/mL) in our cohort. The diagnostic accuracy assessment further demonstrated a clear advantage for serum sPD-L1 over conventional biomarkers in terms of discriminative capacity: CEA (AUC = 0.730, 95% CI = 0.661–0.799, p < 0.001), CA15-3 (AUC = 0.743, 95% CI = 0.675–0.812, p < 0.001), and non-significant CA125 (AUC = 0.533, 95% CI = 0.449–0.616, p = 0.417) (Fig. 4B–D). Using the established clinical thresholds (CEA: 5 ng/mL; CA15-3: 20 U/mL; CA125: 47 U/mL), sPD-L1 showed balanced sensitivity (62.2%) and specificity (73.4%), while CEA and CA15-3 exhibited characteristically low sensitivity (20.7% and 21.2%) despite high specificity (99.2% and 98.4%). Notably, combining sPD-L1 with CEA and CA15-3 yielded enhanced diagnostic performance (AUC = 0.774, 95% CI = 0.705–0.843, p < 0.001, Fig. 4E) compared to individual biomarkers, establishing sPD-L1 as both an independent diagnostic biomarker and a valuable complement to existing biomarker panels for breast cancer detection.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis of breast cancer status and staging
We established two multivariate logistic regression models to assess the independent predictive value of sPD-L1. Model 1 (Table S2) utilized BC status (case = 1, control = 0) as the dependent variable. sPD-L1 remained an independent predictor of BC status after adjusting for age and inflammatory markers (Adjusted OR = 2.117, 95% CI = 1.457–3.074,   p < 0.001). This finding aligned with the previous correlation analysis. Model 2 (Table S3) analyzed disease stage in the BC patient subgroup (advanced [III-IV] = 1, early [I-II] = 0). The association between sPD-L1 and disease progression was no longer significant after we adjusted for age, lymph node status, Ki-67, and histological subtype (p = 0.499). This result differed from the correlation analysis, potentially due to the insufficient sample size in this study. This limitation presented difficulty for identifying the independent effect of sPD-L1 in the multivariate model by reducing statistical power, increasing model instability, and amplifying multicollinearity issues.

Concordant secretion of sPD-L1 and IL-6 in cell culture systems and peripheral blood
Our in vitro investigations revealed significant differences in secretory profiles between normal and malignant breast cells, with MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells demonstrating markedly higher sPD-L1 production compared to MCF-10A normal mammary epithelial cells (p = 0.001). The specific rationale for selecting MDA-MB-231 cells was detailed in the Supporting Information (Fig. S3). This secretory capacity was further enhanced when MCF-10A cells were co-cultured with MDA-MB-231 cells (p = 0.017, Fig. 5A and S4).

IL-6 stands out among cancer-related cytokines for its well-documented contributions to breast cancer metastasis and immune suppression [12]. Parallel analysis of IL-6 showed similar secretion patterns: MDA-MB-231 cells produced substantially more IL-6 than MCF-10A cells (p < 0.001), with co-culture conditions significantly amplifying IL-6 release compared to MCF-10A monoculture (p < 0.001, Fig. 5B). PBMC co-culture experiments demonstrated that both HD- and BC patient-derived PBMCs secreted significantly higher levels of sPD-L1 (HD: p = 0.007; BC: p = 0.002) and IL-6 (HD: p < 0.001; BC: p < 0.001) when cultured with MDA-MB-231 versus MCF-10A cells (Fig. 5C and D). Notably, we observed a strong positive correlation (r = 0.885, 95% CI = 0.756–0.947, p < 0.001, Fig. 5E) between sPD-L1 and IL-6 concentrations across all culture conditions. We further measured serum IL-6 levels in our cohort to appraise their association with sPD-L1. The median serum IL-6 concentration was significantly higher in BC patients (0.006 ng/mL, range: 0.002–0.524) compared to the HD (0.004 ng/mL, range: 0.001–0.015, p < 0.001) and BBD groups (0.004 ng/mL, range: 0.002–0.140, p < 0.001, Figure S5). Besides, Spearman correlation analysis demonstrated a significant positive correlation between serum sPD-L1 and IL-6 levels (r = 0.147, 95% CI = 0.012–0.287, p = 0.028, Fig. 5F), which implied a close association between increased sPD-L1 and systemic inflammatory responses. This supported the regulatory mechanism of sPD-L1 in the breast cancer-associated inflammatory microenvironment and confirmed the potential of sPD-L1 as a clinically relevant biomarker.

To investigate the correlation between sPD-L1 and IL-6 and its potential regulatory mechanisms, we employed siRNA to knock down PD-L1 expression in MDA-MB-231 cells. RT-qPCR and western blot analyses confirmed a significant decrease in PD-L1 mRNA and protein levels in transfected cells (Fig. S6). We then measured sPD-L1 and IL-6 secretion levels in these PD-L1 siRNA-transfected MDA-MB-231 cells. PD-L1 gene silencing significantly reduced sPD-L1 secretion (p = 0.005) and led to a synchronous decrease in IL-6 secretion (p = 0.03) levels (Fig. 6A and B). We co-cultured these cells with MCF-10A cells and PBMCs to study this regulatory relationship during intercellular communication. Secretion of sPD-L1 (p = 0.03) and IL-6 (p = 0.007) significantly declined in the MCF-10A co-culture system dominated by PD-L1-silenced MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 6C and D). The PBMC co-culture system replicated this phenomenon (Fig. 6E and F). These data suggested that PD-L1 expression in breast cancer cells governed sPD-L1 release and actively regulated IL-6 secretion, which verified the synergistic expression pattern of sPD-L1 and IL-6 in the breast cancer microenvironment.

Furthermore, we evaluated how sPD-L1 effected the migration and proliferation capabilities of MDA-MB-231 cells. Silencing PD-L1 significantly reduced the proliferation activity of MDA-MB-231 cells, as presented in Fig. 7A (p = 0.001). The migration capacity was significantly reduced simultaneously, and the 24-h migration rate decreased by approximately 37% (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7B and C). These results indicated that sPD-L1 may promote cellular migration and proliferation, thereby contributing to breast cancer progression. This evidence supported the synergistic role of sPD-L1 and IL-6 in breast cancer biology and reinforced the potential of sPD-L1 as a clinically relevant biomarker.

# Discussion
Despite substantial advancements in treatment outcomes evidenced by improved five-year survival rates, breast cancer incidence continues to escalate globally [13], underscoring the critical need for novel predictive biomarkers that are not only cost-effective and easily accessible but also capable of improving screening efficacy and providing reliable clinical support for patient management.

Tumor cells exploit immune checkpoint pathways, particularly the PD-L1 axis, to foster immune tolerance and escape within TME [4]. Beyond membrane-bound PD-L1, the soluble form (sPD-L1) has emerged as a clinically relevant mediator, with elevated levels documented across multiple malignancies including lung, hepatocellular, gastric, and renal cell carcinomas, as well as melanoma [8]. Our findings aligned with and extend this evidence, demonstrating significantly higher circulating sPD-L1 concentrations in BC patients compared to both BBD controls and healthy individuals, confirming its diagnostic discriminative capacity. Multivariate logistic regression analysis further indicated sPD-L1 as an independent predictor of breast cancer status. Mechanistically, PD-L1 contributes to tumorigenesis and progression through multifaceted pathways: it exerts pro-tumorigenic effects by suppressing the STAT3/caspase-7 axis and neutralizing IFN-mediated cytotoxicity, while its cytoplasmic domain critically regulates protein stability and immunomodulatory functions [14]. Our further exploration revealed that sPD-L1 levels correlated with disease aggressiveness, showing significant elevations in advanced-stage (vs early-stage) and distant metastatic (vs non-distant metastatic) patients, though no associations were observed with lymph node metastasis or Ki-67 index. These results not only corroborate prior studies but also highlight sPD-L1’s potential as a dynamic biomarker for predicting disease progression and metastatic risk in breast cancer.

The systemic inflammatory response, characterized by circulating immune cells (monocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, platelets) and their derived ratios (LMR, NLR, PLR), plays a fundamental role in tumor development and progression [15]. Of these components, lymphocytes serve as critical mediators of anti-tumor immunity through their ability to inhibit tumor proliferation and invasion, induce cytotoxic cell death, and facilitate tumor clearance [16, 17]. Our findings demonstrated significant inverse correlations between circulating sPD-L1 levels and both absolute lymphocyte count and LMR, supporting the hypothesis that sPD-L1 contributes to an immunosuppressive TME by enabling tumor cells to evade T-cell-mediated immunosurveillance. Additionally, it can also be a side effect to explain the association of sPD-L1 with distant metastasis. Conversely, we observed positive correlations between sPD-L1 and both NLR and PLR. Monocytes release a variety of cytokines, matrix metalloproteinases and growth factors, all of which are associated with vascular remodeling, tumor growth and distant metastasis [18]. Within the TME, PD-L1 upregulation increases monocyte surface PD-L1 expression and enhances inflammatory cytokine secretion [19]. This may explain the positive correlation between sPD-L1 and NLR. Whereas platelet-mediated facilitation of primary tumor growth, circulating tumor cell adhesion, colonization, and immune escape may account for the positive correlation between sPD-L1 and PLR [20].

Current clinical practice utilizes several biomarkers, including CEA, CA15-3, and CA125, for breast cancer screening. Our findings revealed important limitations of these conventional biomarkers: while CEA and CA15-3 demonstrated diagnostic utility in differentiating benign from malignant breast disease, they showed no significant association with tumor stage progression, and CA125 failed to distinguish between healthy and diseased populations altogether. In contrast, serum sPD-L1 levels exhibited a progressive increase with advancing tumor stage, indicating that it closely tracks disease progression. Notably, correlation analysis revealed significant positive associations between sPD-L1 and all three traditional biomarkers, indicating that sPD-L1 may serve not only as a standalone biomarker but also as a valuable complement to existing biomarkers in comprehensive diagnostic panels. This potential was further supported by ROC curve analysis, where sPD-L1 demonstrated a higher AUC value and more balanced sensitivity/specificity profiles compared to conventional biomarkers, positioning it as a promising candidate for improving current breast cancer screening and diagnosis paradigms.

Our in vitro experiments demonstrated significantly higher sPD-L1 secretion by MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells compared to MCF-10A mammary epithelial cells, with this effect being further amplified during their co-culture. The dominance of sPD-L1 originating from MDA-MB-231 cells indicates that tumor cells are the major contributors of sPD-L1 in the breast cancer microenvironment. To better recapitulate the physiological TME, we extended these findings using PBMC co-culture systems, where PBMCs from both HD and BC patients exhibited increased sPD-L1 release when cultured with breast cancer cells versus normal mammary epithelial cells. These aligned closely with our clinical serum sPD-L1 measurements. We propose that elevated sPD-L1 production facilitates cancer immune evasion through enhanced binding to lymphocyte PD-1 receptors, thereby suppressing lymphocyte activation and proliferation. This mechanistic model provides a plausible explanation for the observed inverse relationship between circulating sPD-L1 levels and absolute lymphocyte count in our clinical data.

IL-6, a pleiotropic cytokine with both pro- and anti-inflammatory properties, plays pivotal roles in immune regulation and cancer development [12, 21, 22]. Emerging evidence demonstrates complex interactions between IL-6 and PD-L1 signaling. Chan et al. revealed that IL-6-mediated JAK1 activation induced PD-L1 Tyr112 phosphorylation, promoting STT3A-dependent glycosylation and stabilization of PD-L1, with clinical correlations observed in hepatocellular carcinoma [23]. Furthermore, Bent et al. showed that IL-6 counteracted PD-L1 blockade efficacy by upregulating PD-1 expression on T cells, predisposing to T cell exhaustion [24]. Our in vitro studies extended these findings to breast cancer, demonstrating parallel overexpression patterns of IL-6 and sPD-L1—both were significantly elevated in breast cancer cell cultures compared to normal mammary epithelial cells, with further increases observed in co-culture systems. This coordinated expression was recapitulated in PBMC co-cultures from HD and BC patients, mirroring our clinical serum findings. The strong positive correlation between sPD-L1 and IL-6 concentrations in both cell culture and peripheral blood, which persisted following PD-L1 knockdown, suggested their synergistic action within the TME. This synery may collectively promote T cell dysfunction, immune evasion, and tumor progression. These mechanistic insights substantially strengthen the rationale for investigating sPD-L1 as a diagnostic biomarker for breast cancer in clinical application.

Limitations and future perspectives
We systematically evaluated the diagnostic value of circulating sPD-L1 as a TME-derived biomarker in breast cancer and proposed clinically practical “rule-in” and “rule-out” thresholds, though certain limitations remain.

The study’s sample size and design represent the primary limitations. Patient sources in this single-center cross-sectional study were relatively homogeneous, which may influence results through specific population characteristics and selection bias, thereby limiting the generalizability of findings. Additionally, the limited sample size resulted in a wide confidence interval for the identified cut-off value and indicated room for improved accuracy. This constraint also limited statistical power in subgroup analyses and explained the discrepancies between multivariate logistic regression and correlation analyses for breast cancer staging. Besides, the cross-sectional design explained the correlation between sPD-L1 and disease status at only a single time point and couldn’t track dynamic changes during disease progression or treatment response.

Technical variability in sPD-L1 detection constitutes a second significant concern, as no unified standardized protocol currently exists for measuring circulating sPD-L1. Differences in detection platforms (e.g., various commercial ELISA kits), sample types (serum vs. plasma), and processing protocols directly influence results. These factors present obstacles to the direct transfer and application of our reported cut-off values across different laboratories.

Future research must conduct large-scale, multicenter, prospective cohort studies to overcome these limitations and advance sPD-L1 toward clinical translation. These studies should enroll unselected populations undergoing breast cancer screening or diagnosis from diverse geographic regions and healthcare facilities. A prospective design ensures standardized procedures from sample collection to sPD-L1 detection and offers the highest level of evidence for diagnostic utility. Large-scale cohorts will also offer sufficient statistical power to verify the proposed “rule-in” and “rule-out” thresholds and support in-depth analyses of different subgroups. These efforts will finally pave the pathway for sPD-L1 to become a robust, reliable, and widely applicable non-invasive diagnostic tool.

Conclusions
Compared to conventional biomarkers, circulating sPD-L1 demonstrates enhanced sensitivity while preserving balanced specificity, establishing its dual utility as a predictive biomarker for breast cancer diagnosis and a potential indicator of disease progression and distant metastatic risk. Its strong correlation with key immune-inflammatory markers, including IL-6, further underscores its biological relevance and diagnostic advantage, offering improved accuracy and reliability in clinical assessments. To fully validate its clinical applicability in breast cancer diagnostic discrimination and longitudinal monitoring, large-scale multicenter studies are warranted to further explore its value.
