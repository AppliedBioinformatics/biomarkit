# Introduction
Cancer treatment has evolved through distinct therapeutic generations, each addressing limitations of preceding approaches. First-generation chemotherapy drugs directly attack cancer cells but also target normal cells, causing significant side effects [1]. Second-generation targeted therapies, such as Herceptin (trastuzumab), were developed to address these issues [2], maximizing effectiveness while reducing toxicity [3]. However, targeted therapies face challenges including variability in target expression among individuals [4] and potential drug resistance development [5]. Third-generation immuno-oncology approaches aim to enhance the body's immune system to combat cancer [6]. These include immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting PD-1, PD-L1, and CTLA-4 that have gained clinical approval [7], and immune cell therapies like CAR-T and CAR-NK under active development [8]. Oncolytic viruses, due to their mechanism of action largely involving immune enhancement, are also classified within immuno-oncology therapeutics [9].

Oncolytic viruses exploit the natural viral life cycle, wherein viruses replicate by entering host cells, hijacking cellular machinery to produce viral particles, and eventually lysing cells to release new viruses for further infection [10]. These viruses are engineered to selectively infect and kill cancer cells [11]. Based on viral type, oncolytic viruses include herpesvirus-based, adenovirus-based, and vaccinia virus-based platforms [12], with poliovirus, measles virus, vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), and reovirus also under investigation [13]. Infected cancer cells become viral factories, with host cell growth halted or resources redirected for viral reproduction, ultimately destroying cancerous cells [14].

Among oncolytic platforms, adenovirus-based oncolytic viruses are extensively studied [15]. Adenoviruses are non-enveloped, double-stranded DNA viruses engineered to selectively replicate within and lyse cancer cells while sparing normal cells [11]. Their large genome accommodates therapeutic gene insertion, enhancing oncolytic potential or modulating the tumor microenvironment for immune-mediated tumor destruction [16]. Key advantages include strong anti-tumor immune response induction through direct oncolysis and tumor-associated antigen release during cell lysis [14], helping overcome immunosuppressive tumor environments [17]. Adenovirus-based vectors have been engineered to target specific tumor markers or express pro-apoptotic or immune-stimulating genes, enhancing specificity and effectiveness across various cancers [18]. This versatility and safety profile make adenoviruses promising platforms for oncolytic therapies, particularly for treatment-resistant cancers.

BZLF1-based oncolytic approaches have garnered special attention. BZLF1, an EBV immediate-early gene, serves as a key transcriptional activator of the EBV lytic cycle and promotes host cell death by inducing lytic reactivation in EBV latently infected cells [19, 20]. Kenney's group first developed a defective adenovirus (Ad-Z) expressing BZLF1 under the CMV promoter [19]. While Ad-Z demonstrated high BZLF1 expression in EBV-positive cells, it showed limitations in inducing EBV lytic reactivation in C18 nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) tumors and restricted tumor suppression effects even with ganciclovir combination therapy. These results confirmed BZLF1's therapeutic potential while highlighting the need for more effective delivery systems.

Concurrently, the human telomerase reverse transcriptase promoter (hTERTp) has gained attention for developing tumor-selective replicating oncolytic adenoviruses. hTERT is overexpressed in most cancer cells while rarely expressed in normal cells, making it an ideal promoter for tumor-selective gene expression [21, 22]. Hoffman's group developed oncolytic adenovirus OBP-301, which uses hTERTp to regulate E1A-E1B genes, demonstrating effective replication and tumor suppression in pancreatic, liver, and lung cancers [22]. These studies demonstrated the clinical utility of hTERTp-based oncolytic viruses.

Combining these complementary approaches, we developed Ad-TBZ, a novel oncolytic adenovirus containing both an hTERTp-regulated E1A-E1B cassette and a CMV promoter-regulated BZLF1 gene. Ad-TBZ simultaneously provides selective replication capability in cancer cells and lytic reactivation induction capability in EBV-positive tumor cells, targeting EBV-associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC). This study demonstrates Ad-TBZ's potential to overcome limitations of previous single approaches and provide an effective therapeutic strategy for EBVaGC.

# Materials and methods
Cell cultures and reagents
AGS-EBV is a gastric carcinoma cell (GC) line derived from AGS cells infected with a recombinant Akata virus [23]. MKN1-EBV were generated by stably transfecting EBV bacmid. AGS (GC, EBV−), AGS-EBV (GC, EBV+), MKN1-EBV (GC, EBV+), SNU719 (GC, EBV+), and NCC24 (GC, EBV+) cells were maintained in RPMI1640 (Hyclone, Logan, UT, USA). YCCEL1 (GC, EBV+) cells were grown in EMEM (Sigma–Aldrich, MO, USA). The normal human dermal fibroblast cell line CCD-986sk was purchased from Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Korea) and cultured in the medium recommended by the manufacturer. All media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone) and antibiotics (Gibco, Waltham, MA, USA). All cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Production of recombinant adenoviruses
Non-replicative and oncolytic adenoviruses were constructed using the ViraPower™ Adenoviral Gateway™ Expression Kit (Invitrogen, K4930-00). The pAd/CMV/V5-DEST™ vector produces serotype 5 adenoviruses (Ad5) lacking E1A, E1B, and E3 genes, expressing genes of interest under the human cytomegalovirus promoter (CMVp, 584 bp). Using this pAd/CMVp/V5-DEST™ vector, we first generated two defective adenoviruses: Ad5-CMVp-LacZ (Ad-LacZ, Fig. 1a) as a control virus and Ad5-CMVp-BZLF1 (Ad-BZ, Fig. 1b) for BZLF1 expression. For Ad-BZ construction, the BZLF1 gene (946 bp) was cloned into the pENTR3C vector to create pENTR3C-BZLF1. Through Gateway® LR clonase reaction between pENTR3C-BZLF1 and the pAd/CMVp/V5-DEST™ vector, we generated pAd/CMVp/V5-GW/BZLF1, where BZLF1 was inserted into the adenoviral E1 region with a 149 bp spacer between CMVp and BZLF1.

Construction of oncolytic virus
We constructed three oncolytic vectors. A 470 bp fragment of the human TERT promoter (hTERTp) was amplified by PCR from homologous recombination (HR)-hTERTp (Addgene plasmid #71398) using specifically designed primers: SalI_hTERTp-E1A-IRES-E1B_F [CCA GCG TCG ACA GGG CCT CCA CAT CAT GGC CCC TCC CTC GGG] and NotI_hTERTp-E1A-IRES-E1B_R [ATA AGA ATG CGG CCG CTC AAT CTG TAT CTT CAT CGC TAG AGC CA]. The amplified hTERTp product was purified using gel extraction with the Wizard® SV Gel and PCR Clean-Up System (Promega, #A9282), subcloned into the pGEM-T Vector (Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and subsequently digested with SalI and NotI restriction enzymes. The resulting hTERTp fragment was inserted into the adenovirus shuttle plasmid, pENTR3C (SalI, NotI-digested). The pENTR3C vector containing hTERTp was recombined with the adenoviral vector pAd/PL-DEST to generate Ad5-hTERTp-E1A-IRES-E1B (Ad-T, Fig. 1c), which contains hTERTp (470 bp)-driven E1A (986 bp) and E1B (1796 bp) genes connected by IRES (574 bp). Ad5-hTERTp-E1A-IRES-E1B-poly(A) (Ad-T-pA, Fig. 1d) was subsequently created by adding HSV TK poly(A) signal (49 bp) to the 3′ end of E1B in the Ad-T construct. Finally, the therapeutic oncolytic virus Ad5-hTERTp-E1A-IRES-E1B-CMVp-BZLF1 (Ad-TBZ, Fig. 1e) was constructed by inserting the hTERTp-E1A-IRES-E1B cassette upstream of the CMVp-BZLF1 construct with a 224 bp spacer between E1B and CMVp. The final construct, pAd/hTERTp/E1/CMVp/V5-DEST™/BZLF1, enables selective replication in cancer cells while expressing BZLF1. All adenoviral vectors were linearized by PacI digestion to expose the inverted terminal repeats (ITRs) and purified by ethanol precipitation. HEK293 or HEK293A cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen). After 24 h, the culture medium was replaced with fresh DMEM. At 48 h post-transfection, cells were trypsinized and transferred to larger tissue culture plates (10 cm or 100 mm) and cultured until 80% cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed. Cells and media were collected, and crude viral lysates were prepared through 2–3 freeze–thaw cycles in a – 80 °C freezer and a 37 °C water bath. For amplification, HEK293 cells were infected with 2 PFU of adenovirus in 150 mm tissue culture plates. The adenovirus was released from the cell pellet and purified using the ViraBind Adenovirus Miniprep Kit (CELL BIOLABS, #VPK-099) following the manufacturer's protocol. Viral titers were quantified using the Adeno-X Rapid Titer Kit (Invitrogen).

Luciferase assay
SNU719-BHLF1 cells were specifically engineered to link the BHLF1 promoter to a reporter gene, allowing assessment of EBV lytic gene activity using luciferase reporter assays. SNU719-BHLF1 cells were seeded at 30,000 cells per well in 96-well plates. After 24 h, cells were infected with Ad-LacZ, Ad-BZ, and Ad-TBZ at multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 20 and 50 for 48 h. At 48 h post-infection, luciferase activities were measured using the Bright-Glo luciferase assay system (Promega), following manufacturer's instructions.

Cell viability assay
Cell viability assays were performed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Kumamoto, Japan) according to manufacturer's protocol and analyzed using an Infinite 200 pro TECAN spectrophotometer (TECAN, Männedorf, Switzerland) at 450 nm. Cell lines were inoculated in 96-well plates at 10,000 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h. Cells were infected with oncolytic viruses at multiplicities of infection (MOIs) ranging from 3 to 300. For combination assays, cells were treated with oncolytic viruses, cisplatin, or ganciclovir (GCV) at indicated doses. After 48 h treatment, cells were incubated with 10 μL CCK-8 reagent for 4 h. Optical density (OD) values were measured at 450 nm.

Cytotoxicity assay
SNU719 cells were seeded at 4.5 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates and cultured overnight at 37 °C. Cells were treated with Ad-TBZ at MOI 40 (100 μL/well) for combination treatment groups, while control groups received equal volumes of medium. After 48 h incubation, medium was removed and cells were treated with cisplatin (0–532.48 μM) or oxaliplatin (0–514 μM) at various concentrations for an additional 48 h for combination treatment groups (cisplatin + Ad-TBZ and oxaliplatin + Ad-TBZ). Monotherapy groups received cisplatin or oxaliplatin alone at identical concentration gradients. Cell cytotoxicity was assessed using CCK-8 assay. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Apoptosis assay
Cell apoptosis was determined using the Muse® Annexin V & Dead Cell Assay Kit (Millipore, MA, USA) according to manufacturer's instructions. After oncolytic virus treatment for 2 days, cells were harvested using trypsin–EDTA, washed twice with PBS, and stained with Muse® Annexin V & Dead Cell reagents (Millipore). Stained cells were incubated at room temperature for 20 min in darkness. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Muse® Cell Analyzer (Millipore), and cells were classified into live, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic populations.

Western blotting
Cells were harvested after treatment at indicated times, washed once with PBS, and lysed in Pro-Prep (InTron Inc., Seoul, Korea). Proteins were loaded onto SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, MA, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk at room temperature for 1 h, incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, then with secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Membranes were visualized using Clarity Western ECL Substrate (Bio-Rad). Antibodies used: anti-BZLF1 (SC-53904, Santa Cruz, TX, USA), anti-BMRF1 (SC-517584, Santa Cruz), anti-E1A (SC-58658, Santa Cruz), anti-β-actin (SC-69879, Santa Cruz), anti-BRLF1 (BS-4542R, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA), anti-BALF4 (SC-56980, Santa Cruz), anti-BLLF1 (SC-56981, Santa Cruz), secondary HRP-linked anti-rabbit IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific), secondary HRP-linked anti-mouse IgG (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Cytopathic effect (CPE) assay
SNU719 cells (5 × 105 cells/well) and MKN1-EBV cells (5 × 105 cells/well) were cultured in 24-well plates for 24 h at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. Cells were treated with serial dilutions (MOI 0–300) of defective adenovirus (Ad-LacZ, Ad-BZ) or oncolytic adenoviruses (Ad-T, Ad-T-pA, and Ad-TBZ) and incubated for 96 h. After PBS washing, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 h, washed again, and stained with 1% crystal violet in 20% ethanol for 20 min. Following washing and air-drying, staining intensity was measured using ImageJ. Conversion values were calculated by subtracting measured values from 200, with the highest value normalized to 1 to determine relative CPE.

Animal experiment 1
Male nude mice (5 weeks, Hana Biotech) were housed in pathogen-free conditions (23–27 °C, 12 h light/dark). MKN1-EBV cells (7 × 10⁶) were implanted subcutaneously. When tumors reached 100 mm3 volume, mice were divided into two groups (n = 5) receiving intratumoral Ad-LacZ or Ad-TBZ (1 × 10⁸ PFU/mouse, 6 injections over 19 days). Tumor size was calculated as (length × width2)/2. Statistical analysis used Student's t-test. All procedures were approved (permit: 2023-0518).

Animal experiment 2
Male NSG mice (7 weeks, Hana Biotech) were housed in controlled conditions. SNU719 cells (5 × 10⁶) in PBS/50% Matrigel were injected subcutaneously. At 100 mm3, tumors received Ad-LacZ, Ad-T, or Ad-TBZ (5 × 107 PFU/mouse, 10 injections over 32 days). Tumor tissues underwent immunohistochemical analysis with anti-EBV BZLF1 and anti-adenovirus E1A antibodies. Statistical comparisons used one-way ANOVA with Tukey's test (permit: 2024–0523).

Animal experiment 3
Male NSG mice (7 weeks, Hana Biotech) were housed in standard conditions. SNU719 cells (5 × 10⁶) were injected as above. At 100 mm3, mice were divided into four groups: Ad-LacZ, Ad-TBZ (1 × 10⁸ PFU/mouse, 5 injections), oxaliplatin (5 mg/kg, 3 injections), or combination therapy over 23 days. Tissues underwent histological analysis and Western blot for BZLF1 and E1A expression. Statistical analysis used one-way ANOVA with Tukey's test (permit: 2023–0057).

Histology and immunohistochemistry (IHC)
Tumor tissues (n = 2 per group) were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS (pH 7.4) for 36 h, dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (3 μm) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Harris hematoxylin and Eosin Y Alcoholic, BBC Biochemical). For nuclear clarity, sections were differentiated in 0.5% HCl and blued in 0.05% ammonia water. For IHC, antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer, followed by blocking with H₂O₂ and 5% normal goat serum. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-BZLF1 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-53904, 1:200) or anti-E1A (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; sc-58658, 1:300), and then with HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (EnVision Kit; Agilent Technologies; K4001) for 45 min at room temperature. Signals were visualized using DAB substrate (Dako, Agilent Technologies) and counterstained with Mayer’s hematoxylin (BBC Biochemical). Slides were scanned with Axioscan 7 (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany), analyzed at 20 × magnification using ZEN 3.10 software, and quantified with ImageJ to determine the ratio of stained area to total tissue area.

Ethics approval
All animal experiments were performed in accordance with protocols approved by institutional animal care committees. Xenograft models were approved by the Animal Experiments Committee of Kyungpook National University (permit 2023-0518) and the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (permits 2024-0523 and 2023-0057). All procedures followed institutional guidelines for laboratory animal care and use.

Statistical analysis
Experiments were performed at least three times. Statistical analyses used GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Prism 10.3.0, San Diego, CA, USA). Two-tailed Student's t-tests assessed differences between two groups, while one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test compared three or more groups. Statistical significance is indicated by asterisks (*) for t-tests and different letters for ANOVA with Tukey's test. Data are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. Different letters indicate statistically significant differences (P < 0.05).

# Results
Construction and functional characterization of BZLF1-expressing oncolytic adenoviruses in EBVaGC cells
We successfully generated five adenoviral constructs: Ad-LacZ (control), Ad-BZ (BZLF1-expressing), Ad-T (oncolytic), Ad-T-pA (oncolytic with poly(A)), and Ad-TBZ (therapeutic oncolytic expressing BZLF1).

To evaluate functional efficacy, we assessed BZLF1 gene expression in EBVaGC cell lines following Ad-TBZ infection. Cells were infected with Ad-TBZ at MOIs of 20 and 50 for 48 h, and RT-qPCR analysis measured BZLF1 transcript levels relative to ACTB.

High levels of BZLF1 transcription were observed in SNU719 (Fig. 1f), AGS-EBV (Fig. 1g) and NCC24 (Fig. 1h) cells. YCCEL1 cells (Fig. 1i) also exhibited significant BZLF1 upregulation, though the relative expression values appeared lower due to differences in baseline β-actin expression levels across cell lines. Critically, SNU719 cells, which were subsequently used in xenograft tumor models, demonstrated robust BZLF1 induction following Ad-TBZ infection at MOI 50, with significantly higher transcript levels compared to both Ad-LacZ and Ad-BZ controls (Fig. 1f). This confirms effective BZLF1 expression in the clinically relevant cell line used for in vivo therapeutic validation.

Western blot analysis examined E1A and BZLF1 protein expression. Cells were infected with Ad-BZ or Ad-TBZ at MOIs of 10, 50, and 100 for 48 h. Both E1A and BZLF1 proteins were more strongly expressed in SNU719 (Fig. 1i) and YCCEL1 (Fig. 1k) cells at higher MOIs, whereas NCC24 cells (Fig. 1j) exhibited robust protein expression even at lower MOIs.

BZLF1 expression was also evaluated in MKN1-EBV cells. Western blot analysis showed that both Ad-BZ and Ad-TBZ induced robust BZLF1 protein expression at MOIs of 10, 50, and 100. Ad-TBZ showed elevated E1A protein expression specifically at MOI 100 (Online Resource 1a). RT-qPCR confirmed substantial BZLF1 transcript expression in MKN1-EBV cells compared to Ad-LacZ control (Online Resource 1b). Expression of other EBV lytic genes (BRLF1, BMRF1, BGLF4, BLLF1) was significantly increased by both Ad-BZ and Ad-TBZ (Online Resource 1c).

Cell viability assays using the Muse® Count & Viability Assay Kit showed that Ad-TBZ reduced MKN1-EBV cell viability compared to controls at MOI 20 (74.2% vs 84.9% for Ad-LacZ) and MOI 50 (69.2% vs 84.0% for Ad-LacZ) (Online Resource 1d). Annexin V-based apoptosis analysis revealed increased late apoptotic cells following Ad-TBZ treatment (19.1% and 22.7% at MOIs 20 and 50, respectively) compared to Ad-LacZ control (11.4%) (Online Resource 1e).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that BZLF1-expressing oncolytic adenoviruses can effectively combine viral oncolysis with EBV lytic cycle activation to induce tumor-specific apoptosis in EBVaGC cells, establishing a promising dual-mechanism therapeutic platform for treating EBV-associated gastric cancers.

Selective oncolytic activity of Ad-TBZ in EBVaGC cells
To verify selective replication, we analyzed viral protein expression in CCD-846sk cells (normal colon) and NCC24 cells (EBV-positive gastric cancer). Both cell types were infected with Ad-LacZ, Ad-BZ, or Ad-TBZ at MOI 50 for 48 h. In CCD-846sk cells, E1A protein was barely detectable and BZLF1 protein expression was very low, whereas in NCC24 cells both proteins were robustly expressed (Fig. 2a).

Cell viability assays in CCD-986sk normal fibroblasts showed that Ad-TBZ induced minimal cytotoxicity at MOIs ranging from 0 to 300 for 48 h (Fig. 2b). Similar results were observed with Ad-T and Ad-T-pA (Fig. 2c). In contrast, EBV-positive gastric cancer cells showed distinct cytotoxic effects. Ad-TBZ significantly reduced cell viability in NCC24 cells at MOI 75 (Fig. 2d), YCCEL1 cells at MOI 75 (Fig. 2e), and SNU719 cells at MOI 225 (Fig. 2f). Across different EBVaGC cell lines, varying MOIs of Ad-TBZ were required to achieve cytotoxic effects, likely due to differences in CAR expression levels and EBV latency stages between cell lines.

To further elucidate the mechanism underlying Ad-TBZ-induced cytotoxicity in EBVaGC cells, we investigated whether cell death was mediated by late apoptosis—a hallmark of lytic replication–induced cell lysis [24, 25]. Oncolytic viruses expressing BZLF1 enable both adenoviral and EBV lytic replication cycles in EBVaGC cells, ultimately leading to host cell death and lysis. To assess this, we examined the percentage of cells undergoing late apoptosis in EBVaGC cell populations infected with Ad-TBZ for 48 h using the Muse Annexin V & Dead Cell Assay.

Late apoptosis analysis using the Muse Annexin V & Dead Cell Assay showed cell line-specific responses. In NCC24 cells, approximately 80% or more cells were in late apoptosis at MOIs 20 and 50 (Fig. 2g). YCCEL1 cells exhibited 40–50% late apoptotic cells (Fig. 2h), while SNU719 cells showed minimal late apoptosis (< 5%) at MOIs 20 and 50, increasing to approximately 10% at MOI 200 (Fig. 2i). The differential apoptotic responses observed across cell lines, particularly the lower late apoptosis rates in SNU719 cells, can be attributed to varying degrees of hypermethylation that create different epigenetic barriers to EBV lytic reactivation.

Ad-TBZ demonstrated selective cytotoxicity in EBV-positive gastric cancer cells with minimal effects on normal cells. These findings indicate that Ad-TBZ achieves cancer cell selectivity through three complementary mechanisms: hTERTp-mediated selective adenoviral replication in cancer cells, minimal activity in normal cells due to low hTERTp activation, and enhanced BZLF1 expression that effectively induces EBV lytic reactivation in EBV-positive cells.

BZLF1-mediated oncolysis in EBVaGC cells
This oncolytic virus expresses BZLF1, one of the Immediate-Early (IE) genes that functions as a transcriptional activator within the viral genome. BZLF1 induces the lytic cycle of EBV and promotes the expression of other viral genes necessary for this process. Reactivation of the EBV lytic cycle typically results in the death of the host cell [26]. Based on this mechanism, we hypothesized that BZLF1 protein expression could be leveraged for targeted therapy against EBV-positive gastric cancer cells.

To investigate EBV-specific cytotoxicity, we compared responses in EBV-negative AGS cells and EBV-positive AGS-EBV cells. AGS cells infected with various viruses at MOIs up to 300 showed no statistically significant differences in cytotoxicity (Fig. 3a). In contrast, AGS-EBV cells showed markedly different results. Ad-TBZ demonstrated cytotoxicity differences starting at MOI 3, with statistical significance at MOI 15, and 71.7% higher cytotoxicity compared to Ad-LacZ at MOI 300 (Fig. 3b). Late apoptosis analysis confirmed these findings. AGS cells showed no significant differences between treatments (Fig. 3c), while AGS-EBV cells showed significantly higher late apoptosis rates with Ad-TBZ treatment (Fig. 3d).

Cytopathic effect (CPE) assays in SNU719 cells revealed dose-dependent responses. Ad-LacZ induced approximately 30% CPE at MOI 300, Ad-T induced 35% CPE, Ad-T-pA induced 80% CPE, and Ad-TBZ induced 94% CPE at MOI 300 (Fig. 3e). Cytopathic effect (CPE) assays in MKN1-EBV cells also revealed dose-dependent responses. Ad-LacZ induced approximately 0% CPE at MOI 15, Ad-T induced 0% CPE, Ad-BZ induced 9% CPE, Ad-T-pA induced 52% CPE, and Ad-TBZ induced 93% CPE at MOI 15 (Fig. 3f).

Additional cell viability assessment in SNU719 cells showed that Ad-TBZ infection significantly reduced cell viability in a dose-dependent manner compared to control viruses (Online Resource 2a). At MOI 20, Ad-TBZ-infected SNU719 cells showed significantly reduced viability (71.5%) compared to Ad-LacZ (78.3%, p < 0.05) and Ad-BZ controls. At MOI 200, Ad-TBZ infection resulted in substantially decreased cell viability (63.5%) compared to Ad-LacZ control (84.5%, p < 0.001).

Late apoptosis analysis in SNU719 cells following Ad-TBZ infection revealed dose-dependent apoptotic cell death induction compared to control viruses (Online Resource 2b). At MOI 20, Ad-TBZ-infected SNU719 cells showed significantly increased late apoptosis (1.8%) compared to Ad-LacZ control (0.5%, p < 0.05). At MOI 200, Ad-TBZ infection resulted in substantially increased late apoptosis (9.0%) compared to Ad-LacZ control (2.6%, p < 0.001).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that BZLF1-expressing oncolytic adenoviruses exhibit selective cytotoxicity specifically against EBV-positive gastric cancer cells through EBV lytic cycle reactivation, establishing proof-of-concept for a tumor-specific therapeutic approach that spares EBV-negative normal cells while effectively inducing apoptosis in EBV-associated gastric cancers.

BZLF1-mediated EBV lytic reactivation in EBVaGC cells
To demonstrate that BZLF1 produced by Ad-TBZ induces EBV lytic reactivation, we investigated changes in transcription and translation of genes associated with EBV lytic reactivation in the EBV life cycle, as well as changes in EBV genome copy numbers required for EBV progeny virus production. We tested these effects by infecting EBVaGC cells with our oncolytic virus for 48 h.

First, we examined the transcriptional changes of EBV lytic genes. In AGS-EBV cells, BRLF1, BMRF1, and BGLF4 were upregulated following infection with Ad-TBZ and Ad-BZ at an MOI of 20 (Fig. 4a) for 48 h, while BMRF1, BXLF1, and BALF4 were upregulated following infection at an MOI of 50 (Fig. 4b). Similarly, in NCC24 cells, most of the EBV lytic genes examined were upregulated at an MOI of 20 (Fig. 4c), and the same pattern was observed at MOI of 50 (Fig. 4d). In YCCEL1 cells, BMRF1, BALF4, and BLLF1 were upregulated at an MOI of 20 (Fig. 4e), while BRLF1, BMRF1, BALF4, and BLLF1 were upregulated at an MOI of 50 (Fig. 4f).

To provide comprehensive data for SNU719 cells used in our xenograft model, we also examined EBV lytic gene expression in this clinically relevant cell line. SNU719 cells were infected with Ad-LacZ, Ad-BZ, and Ad-TBZ at MOI 50 for 48 h, and RT-qPCR analysis revealed significant upregulation of most EBV lytic genes following Ad-BZ and Ad-TBZ infection compared to Ad-LacZ control (Fig. 4g). Specifically, BRLF1, BMRF1, BGLF4, BXLF1, BALF4, and BLLF1 showed substantial increases in transcript levels, confirming that Ad-TBZ effectively induces comprehensive EBV lytic reactivation in SNU719 cells, consistent with the patterns observed in other EBV-positive gastric cancer cell lines.

To further validate the lytic reactivation, we utilized SNU719 cells containing a previously published BHLF1 promoter (BHLF1p) and a luciferase-linked BHLF1p construct. These cells were infected with Ad-TBZ at up to an MOI of 10. As expected, Ad-TBZ at an MOI of 5 significantly increased BHLF1p activity, confirming EBV lytic reactivation (Fig. 4h).

To verify that these transcriptional changes resulted in protein expression, we examined the protein levels of EBV lytic genes in EBVaGC cells infected with oncolytic viruses expressing BZLF1 by western blot assay. In NCC24 cells infected with Ad-TBZ for 48 h, BRLF1, BMRF1, and BALF4 proteins were found to be overexpressed (Fig. 4i).

Finally, we investigated changes in EBV genome copy number, a key indicator of successful lytic reactivation and viral replication. In NCC24 cells infected with Ad-TBZ for 48 h, both extracellular and intracellular EBV genome copy numbers were significantly higher compared to the control groups Ad-LacZ and Ad-BZ (Fig. 4j). Similarly, in YCCEL1 cells infected with Ad-TBZ for 48 h, the extracellular and intracellular EBV genome copy numbers were significantly increased compared to the control Ad-LacZ (Fig. 4k). Notably, the EBV genome copy number was higher in YCCEL1 cells than in NCC24 cells.

Collectively, these data demonstrated that BZLF1 expressed by Ad-TBZ effectively induces EBV lytic reactivation in EBVaGC cells through multiple mechanisms: upregulation of key EBV lytic genes and their corresponding proteins, increased BHLF1p activity, and enhanced EBV genome copy numbers. These comprehensive findings support Ad-TBZ's potential as a targeted therapy for EBV-associated gastric carcinoma.

Ad-TBZ suppresses tumor growth in EBVaGC xenograft models
To evaluate the effect of oncolytic viruses expressing BZLF1 on tumor development, we conducted xenograft-based antitumor assays using two EBV-associated gastric carcinoma (EBVaGC) cell lines. First, MKN1-EBV cells were transplanted into nude mice, and 10⁸ PFU of Ad-TBZ was injected into the tumor site three times, starting when the tumor reached a volume of 80 mm3. Tumor volumes in the nude mice were measured over time, with 10⁸ PFU of Ad-LacZ used as a control. On day 21 post-Ad-TBZ injection, tumor volumes were significantly smaller compared to those injected with the control Ad-LacZ (Fig. 5a).

The body weight of nude mice injected with Ad-TBZ remained stable throughout the 19-day experimental period (Online Resource 3a). On day 21, the mice were sacrificed, and the tumor tissues were isolated and weighed. The tumors injected with Ad-TBZ were significantly lighter than those injected with the control (Fig. 5b). Additionally, the length of the isolated tumors was measured; tumors injected with Ad-LacZ measured approximately 20 mm in length, while those injected with Ad-TBZ measured approximately 10 mm (Fig. 5c).

To confirm the expression of viral proteins in the tumor tissues, western blot analysis was performed on MKN1-EBV xenograft tumor samples treated with Ad-LacZ or Ad-TBZ. The analysis revealed that one of the two tumor samples treated with Ad-TBZ showed very strong expression of both E1A and BZLF1 proteins. In contrast, both tumor samples treated with the control Ad-LacZ showed relatively weak BZLF1 protein expression (Fig. 5d). This result confirms the successful delivery and expression of the therapeutic viral proteins in the tumor microenvironment following Ad-TBZ treatment.

Since the tumor suppression effect of Ad-TBZ compared to Ad-LacZ was not complete in the MKN1-EBV cell and nude mouse model, we further validated the antitumor effect using an additional model system with SNU719 cells and NSG mice. We conducted xenograft-based antitumor assays using SNU719 cells, another EBVaGC cell line. A total of 5 × 10⁶ SNU719 cells were mixed with Matrigel matrix and transplanted into NSG mice. When the tumor volume reached 100 mm3, 5 × 107 PFU of Ad-TBZ was injected into the tumor site ten times. Tumor volumes were measured over time, with 5 × 107 PFU of Ad-LacZ used as a control.

The body weight of NSG mice injected with Ad-TBZ remained stable over the 32-day experimental period (Online Resource 3b). In NSG mice, tumor volumes in the Ad-TBZ group began showing statistically significant differences compared to the Ad-LacZ control group from day 21 after the initial oncolytic virus injection. From day 25, the Ad-T group also began showing statistically significant differences in tumor volume compared to the Ad-LacZ group. By day 32 post-injection, the average tumor volume in the Ad-TBZ group was 72.40% of the Ad-LacZ control group, while the Ad-T group's average tumor volume was 76.98% of the control (Fig. 5e).

On day 32, the NSG mice were sacrificed and the tumors were harvested. The weight of the harvested tumors was measured. Compared to tumors injected with Ad-LacZ, tumors injected with Ad-TBZ showed significantly lower weight, while tumors injected with Ad-T showed no statistically significant difference (Fig. 5f). Representative tumors were photographed to document their morphology (Fig. 5g).

Representative tumors from each treatment group (Ad-LacZ, Ad-T, and Ad-TBZ) were selected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), sectioned, mounted on slides, and subjected to DAB staining using BZLF1 and E1A antibodies. Compared to tumor tissue injected with Ad-LacZ, tumor tissue injected with Ad-TBZ showed relatively stronger BZLF1 and E1A signals (Fig. 5h). Quantitative analysis of DAB staining revealed that BZLF1 signaling dots were significantly increased in Ad-TBZ treated tumor tissues compared to Ad-LacZ and Ad-T controls (Fig. 5i, left), while E1A signaling dots showed no significant differences between treatment groups (Fig. 5i, right).

Collectively, these data demonstrated that the oncolytic virus Ad-TBZ effectively inhibits tumor growth in EBVaGC xenograft models. Ad-TBZ significantly reduced tumor volume, weight, and length in both MKN1-EBV and SNU719 tumors compared to the control. The increased expression of BZLF1 and E1A in Ad-TBZ-treated tumors further supports its therapeutic potential for EBV-associated gastric cancer.

In vitro assessment of therapeutic synergy between Ad-TBZ and platinum drugs in SNU719 and AGS-EBV cells
Previous studies have demonstrated synergistic effects of combining oncolytic viruses with antiviral and antitumor agents [27,28,29]. We investigated whether Ad-TBZ exhibits synergistic anticancer effects when combined with platinum-based agents in EBV-positive gastric cancer.

Simultaneous combination therapy with Ad-TBZ (MOI 40) and oxaliplatin for 48 h or 96 h showed no significant differences compared to oxaliplatin monotherapy across most concentration ranges in both SNU719 and AGS-EBV cells (Fig. 6a, b). However, sequential treatment approaches revealed more promising results. When cells were pre-treated with Ad-TBZ for 48 h followed by oxaliplatin treatment, synergistic effects were observed at specific low concentrations (4–8 μM), showing significantly enhanced cytotoxicity compared to oxaliplatin alone in both SNU719 and AGS-EBV cells (Fig. 6c).

Additionally, extended sequential treatment analysis in AGS-EBV cells confirmed synergistic effects at 4–8 μM oxaliplatin concentrations when treatment duration was extended to 96 h (Fig. 6d), demonstrating consistent synergistic patterns across different EBV-positive gastric cancer cell lines and treatment durations.

Combination therapy experiments with cisplatin and Ad-TBZ showed no significant synergistic effects under both simultaneous (48 h and 96 h treatments) and sequential treatment approaches (Online Resource 4a, 4b, 4c). No significant differences were observed between cisplatin monotherapy and cisplatin-Ad-TBZ combination therapy across various concentration ranges, indicating different combination patterns compared to oxaliplatin.

These findings indicate that while modest synergistic effects may occur with oxaliplatin under specific sequential treatment conditions across multiple EBV-positive gastric cancer cell lines, Ad-TBZ demonstrates optimal therapeutic activity primarily as a monotherapy rather than in combination with platinum-based agents.

In vivo evaluation of combined effects of Ad-TBZ with oxaliplatin in SNU719 xenograft model
Based on our in vitro findings that suggested potential synergistic effects specifically between oxaliplatin and Ad-TBZ in both combination and sequential treatment approaches (Fig. 6c, Fig. 6d), we proceeded with in vivo experiments focusing exclusively on oxaliplatin rather than cisplatin for further investigation.

We evaluated the safety and tolerability of Ad-TBZ treatment in our xenograft models by monitoring body weight changes throughout the treatment period. For our in vivo study, NSG mice were subcutaneously injected with SNU719 cells (5 × 106). When tumors reached 100 mm3, mice were treated with Ad-LacZ (control), Ad-TBZ (1 × 108 PFU/mouse, 5 injections), oxaliplatin (5 mg/kg, 3 injections), or their combination (Combi; Ad-TBZ 1 × 108 PFU/mouse, 5 injections plus oxaliplatin 5 mg/kg, 3 injections). Body weight monitoring over the 23-day experimental period showed that all four treatment groups remained stable with no significant differences observed between Ad-LacZ, Ad-TBZ, oxaliplatin, and combination treatment groups (Online Resource 3c). This finding indicates that all treatments were well-tolerated without apparent systemic toxicity in the experimental animals.

Tumor volume was monitored for 23 days post-injection. Notably, the combination treatment (Combi) showed statistically significant reduction in tumor volume compared to both single-agent treatments (Ad-TBZ alone and oxaliplatin alone) and the control (Ad-LacZ) (Fig. 7a). This enhanced anti-tumor effect of the combination therapy became particularly evident from day 14 post-Ad-TBZ injection and continued to strengthen until the end of the experiment at day 23.

Interestingly, despite the clear differences in tumor volume, when tumors were harvested on day 23 post-Ad-TBZ injection, the tumor weights from the combination therapy group (Combi) did not show statistically significant differences compared to those from the single-agent treatment groups (Ad-TBZ alone and oxaliplatin alone) (Fig. 7b). However, all treatment groups showed significantly reduced tumor weights compared to the Ad-LacZ control. Representative tumor images from each treatment group demonstrated the macroscopic differences in tumor size (Fig. 7c).

Histological analysis revealed distinct differences between treatment groups. H&E staining showed vessel-like structures in Ad-LacZ-treated tumors that were absent in Ad-TBZ-treated tumors. DAB staining with anti-adenovirus E1A antibodies showed low levels of E1A protein in Ad-LacZ-treated tumors, while Ad-TBZ-treated tumors exhibited strong and frequent E1A protein expression (Fig. 7d). Quantitative analysis of E1A signaling revealed significantly increased brown dots in Ad-TBZ treated tumor tissues compared to Ad-LacZ controls (Fig. 7e).

Western blot analysis was performed on tumor tissues to further examine protein expression patterns. Total protein was extracted from three tumor samples each from Ad-LacZ- and Ad-TBZ-treated groups. The analysis revealed robust expression of both BZLF1 and E1A proteins in Ad-TBZ-treated tumors. In contrast, tumors treated with Ad-LacZ showed only weak expression of both proteins, likely originating from the SNU719 cells themselves (Fig. 7f). These protein expression data corroborate the immunohistochemistry findings and further confirm successful viral gene delivery and expression in the Ad-TBZ-treated tumors.

These in vivo results confirm our preliminary in vitro observations, demonstrating that the combination of Ad-TBZ with oxaliplatin produces synergistic anti-tumor effects in the SNU719 xenograft model, particularly in reducing tumor volume over time. While this volume reduction did not translate to statistically significant differences in final tumor weights between treatment groups, the combination therapy clearly showed enhanced efficacy in controlling tumor growth throughout the treatment period.

Combination treatment of Ad-TBZ with antiviral agent ganciclovir
Following our investigation of platinum-based drugs, we examined whether combination treatment with the antiviral agent ganciclovir (GCV) could demonstrate synergistic anticancer effects with Ad-TBZ in EBV-positive gastric cancer.

Treatment of SNU719 cells with GCV alone at various concentrations (0–160 μg/mL) for 48 h showed minimal effect on cell viability (Online Resource 5a). Similarly, simultaneous combination therapy with Ad-TBZ and GCV showed no significant synergistic effects compared to monotherapy across tested concentration ranges (Online Resource 5b, 5c).

However, sequential treatment approaches revealed promising results. SNU719 cells pre-treated with Ad-TBZ (MOI 40) for 48 h followed by GCV treatment at various concentrations (0–400 μM) for an additional 48 h showed significantly lower cell viability compared to Ad-TBZ treatment alone across all GCV concentration ranges (Online Resource 5d). This suggested that pre-treatment with Ad-TBZ sensitized the cells to subsequent GCV treatment, demonstrating a synergistic effect in the sequential therapy approach.

We also evaluated whether Ad-TBZ in combination with GCV exhibited similar effects in another EBV-positive gastric cancer cell line, YCCEL1. YCCEL1 cells were treated with Ad-TBZ alone (monotherapy) or in combination with GCV (50 μg/mL) (combination therapy) at MOIs ranging from 0 to 300 for 48 h (Online Resource 5e). Similar to SNU719 cells, the difference in cell viability between the monotherapy and the combination therapy was not statistically significant in almost all MOI ranges, indicating consistent patterns across different EBV-positive gastric cancer cell lines.

To investigate the mechanism underlying the synergistic effects observed in sequential Ad-TBZ and GCV treatment, we examined the expression of viral thymidine kinase (BXLF1) and protein kinase (BGLF4) following various treatment conditions (Online Resource 5f). SNU719 and YCCEL1 cells were treated with GCV (50 μg/mL), Ad-TBZ (MOI 20), or their combination for 48 h, followed by Western blot analysis. The results revealed cell line-specific and protein-specific differences in viral kinase expression patterns. In SNU719 cells, the Ad-TBZ + GCV combination therapy showed enhanced BGLF4 expression compared to monotherapies, while BXLF1 expression in the combination therapy was lower than Ad-TBZ alone but higher than GCV alone or negative control. In YCCEL1 cells, the combination therapy demonstrated enhanced BXLF1 expression compared to Ad-TBZ monotherapy, while BGLF4 expression remained similar between monotherapy and combination therapy (Online Resource 5f). These findings suggest that viral kinase induction varies by cell line and specific proteins, providing limited mechanistic support for the enhanced GCV sensitivity observed in sequential therapy approaches.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that both simultaneous and sequential combination therapies of Ad-TBZ with ganciclovir failed to achieve consistent synergistic effects in EBV-positive gastric cancer cells, with cell line-specific variability in viral kinase expression patterns indicating that the combination approach may have limited therapeutic potential compared to Ad-TBZ monotherapy.

# Discussion
Ad-TBZ represents a novel therapeutic approach that combines hTERTp-mediated selective replication with BZLF1-induced EBV lytic reactivation for EBVaGC treatment.

Our findings demonstrate that Ad-TBZ functions most effectively as a monotherapy, providing optimal therapeutic efficacy through hTERTp-mediated adenoviral lytic cycle induction [30] and BZLF1-mediated EBV lytic reactivation [31]. While combination therapy with platinum-based agents showed modest synergistic effects under specific sequential treatment conditions, monotherapy remains the preferred approach.

The design rationale for Ad-TBZ builds upon established principles in oncolytic virus therapy. Genes that are overexpressed in gastrointestinal cancers, including human TERT, KDR, Bmi-1, Survivin, A33, and CEA, are increasingly being targeted in cancer gene therapy [21]. We selected hTERTp to drive adenoviral early gene expression [15], providing cancer cell selectivity while minimizing normal cell toxicity.

The Ad-TBZ construct incorporates E1A and E1B under hTERTp regulation, while BZLF1 expression is controlled by CMVp. The 224 bp spacer between E1B and CMVp, without a poly(A) signal, may allow RNA polymerase initiating at hTERTp to continue transcribing BZLF1, potentially enhancing therapeutic efficacy.

SNU719 cells, confirmed to be at latency 1 stage with hypermethylation [32], require higher MOI due to repressive epigenetic barriers. This hypermethylated chromatin structure suppresses EBV lytic reactivation, explaining the significantly lower late apoptosis frequency observed in SNU719 cells (≤ 5%) compared to NCC24 and YCCEL1 cells, which demonstrate more readily inducible EBV lytic reactivation.

Combination therapy evaluation revealed limited synergistic potential. While most combination approaches showed no significant synergistic effects, sequential treatment with oxaliplatin demonstrated modest synergistic effects at specific concentrations with cell line-specific patterns (4–8 μM in SNU719 cells and 128 μM in AGS-EBV cells). Sequential GCV treatment showed that Ad-TBZ pre-treatment sensitized EBV-positive gastric cancer cells to subsequent GCV therapy through cell line-specific viral kinase induction (BXLF1 and BGLF4). However, these effects were variable and context-dependent. The limited synergistic potential may be attributed to interference between drug mechanisms and Ad-TBZ replication, consistent with previous findings [33]. These results support Ad-TBZ's optimal function as a monotherapy.

The first BZLF1-expressing adenovirus, Ad-Z, was developed by Kenney's group as a defective adenovirus with BZLF1 under CMV promoter control [19]. While Ad-Z successfully expressed BZLF1 in EBV-positive cells, it demonstrated significant limitations: failure to induce effective EBV lytic reactivation in C18 NPC tumors and lack of synergistic effects with ganciclovir for tumor suppression. These limitations highlighted the need for improved viral replication capacity.

In parallel, Hoffman's group developed OBP-301, a replicating oncolytic adenovirus utilizing hTERTp to drive E1A-E1B expression [22]. OBP-301 demonstrated effective tumor-selective replication and significant tumor suppression in pancreatic, liver, and lung cancers, establishing the clinical utility of hTERTp-based oncolytic approaches. However, OBP-301 lacked EBV-specific targeting mechanisms.

Ad-TBZ represents a rational combination of these complementary approaches, integrating Ad-Z's BZLF1-mediated EBV targeting with OBP-301's hTERTp-driven selective replication. This dual-mechanism design addresses the key limitations of both predecessors: Ad-TBZ achieves robust viral replication through hTERTp selectivity while maintaining EBV-specific lytic reactivation through BZLF1 expression. Our results demonstrate that this combined approach significantly enhances therapeutic efficacy, as evidenced by effective tumor growth inhibition in both in vitro EBVaGC cell lines and in vivo xenograft models.

The success of Ad-TBZ validates the strategic integration of tumor-selective replication with virus-specific targeting, offering a more sophisticated and effective therapeutic platform for EBVaGC treatment compared to single-mechanism approaches.

Clinical translation of Ad-TBZ will require careful consideration of delivery routes. Intratumoral injection represents the most promising approach, utilizing endoscopic guidance similar to current gastric cancer procedures [34]. This method maximizes local viral concentration while minimizing systemic exposure. Alternative routes include intravenous administration for metastatic disease [35], intraperitoneal injection for peritoneal carcinomatosis [36], and intra-arterial delivery for high local concentrations [37]. The optimal route should be determined based on tumor characteristics and patient condition, with endoscopic intratumoral injection being the most practical initial approach for clinical trials.

The findings demonstrate that Ad-TBZ holds significant potential as a targeted oncolytic monotherapy for EBVaGC. These results support the development of Ad-TBZ as a monotherapy agent, offering a novel therapeutic approach for this challenging cancer subtype.
