# Introduction
Africa is the second largest continent and has the most extensive grassland cover in the world (Fig. 1), and its rich grassland resources provide valuable ecosystem services1. Approximately 70–75% of the population relies on rain-fed agriculture and livestock farming for their livelihoods; livestock accounts for approximately 40% of African agricultural GDP, and the sustainable development of the livestock sector has a direct effect on the economic development of African countries as well as food security2,3,4. With the rise of the middle class, African lifestyles are becoming more affluent. Population growth and rising standards of living will lead to increased demand for milk and meat in Africa, which will put additional pressure on livestock production, making a stable supply of pasture essential5.
Overgrazing occurs when livestock numbers exceed grassland carrying capacity. A high stocking rate sustained for over a year due to insufficient capacity triggers a series of adverse effects, including the degradation of the grassland ecosystem, destruction of vegetation cover, and risks to the unhealthy development of grassland animal husbandry6,7. From the perspective of equilibrium/disequilibrium theory, overgrazing can be viewed as a key driver that shifts ecosystems from equilibrium to disequilibrium. Equilibrium theory posits that ecosystems can self-regulate and maintain a dynamic balance under stable conditions8. However, overgrazing disrupts this balance, leading to vegetation degradation, soil erosion, and reduced biodiversity, thereby pushing ecosystems away from their stable states9. Within the framework of disequilibrium theory, the effects of overgrazing are closely tied to climate variability and the frequency of disturbances, exhibiting spatial and temporal heterogeneity: grassland degradation may worsen significantly during drought periods, while partial or full recovery may occur during wet periods8. Implementing rational and efficient grazing management practices can enhance the value of grassland ecosystem services, regulate terrestrial carbon cycles, and improve the ecosystem resilience to natural disasters10. Extensive research now exists to emphasize the importance of avoiding overgrazing stocking11,12,13. However, inefficient utilization of grassland resources will hinder the potential for enhancing economic value, thereby diminishing the productivity and competitiveness of African nations4. Moreover, grassland ecosystems are very fragile, and changes in the grassland carrying capacity are sensitive to weather extremes13, making effective grazing management particularly important. Because of these complex interconnections, further research is needed to determine African countries should increase or decrease the number of grazing animals to ensure sustainability. An accurate assessment of grassland utilization will provide the data needed to support the implementation of livestock management policies.

Grassland carrying capacity is used to assess the ability of grassland resources to sustainably support livestock over the long term and is usually expressed as the maximum number of animals or animal units that can be sustainably accommodated in a single year14,15,16,17. Livestock feeding heavily on grasslands may threaten and damage grassland ecosystems, which is not conducive to the sustainable development of grassland husbandry, thus leading to the risk of unhealthy grassland development. Therefore, determining appropriate stocking rates is particularly important for grassland carrying capacity18,19. There are few relevant studies about grassland carrying capacity in Africa, and existing studies have focused more on the carrying capacity of single pasture species in local areas. These estimation models are not applicable to large-scale grassland carrying capacity estimation studies of multiple grassland types20,21,22. In studies at the global scale, the grassland aboveground biomass (AGB) is usually based on Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) data and uses a single constant of 0.60 as a conversion factor (i.e., AGB = 0.6×NPP)23,24. Some scholars have also directly used the ratio of grazed biomass to aboveground NPP to represent the grass‒livestock balance25. However, not all grassland biomass is available for livestock utilization due to factors such as animal trampling, consumption by other organisms, grass decomposition, and lignification caused by high humidity22. Consequently, these models may produce biased estimations of the grassland carrying capacity. Piipponen et al. optimized the AGB estimation model by using temperature and topographic features and estimated the global-scale potential grassland carrying capacity, which improved the accuracy of estimations of the grassland carrying capacity17. The assessment of grassland carrying capacity, including studies in other regions, currently places greater emphasis on analyzing the state of grassland utilization within a specific year17,20,22. However, changes in grassland carrying capacity are a dynamic process, and their trends over time significantly influence the assessment of grassland carrying capacity sustainability. In addition, there are significant differences in the performance and impacts of the same trends in different countries. For countries with adequate fodder resources, an increase in pressure on grasslands to carry livestock may imply an increase in the efficiency of grassland resource utilization, which in turn drives economic development. However, for countries with insufficient fodder resources, an increase in pressure on grasslands may lead to overconsumption of grassland. Some scholars have assessed this risk based on the fluctuating characteristics of Interannual variation in grassland carrying capacity26. However, the uncertainty in the analytical process and subjective judgment may interfere with the results. Therefore, further research is needed to accurately assess the risk of grassland use. A comprehensive understanding of the risks associated with grassland use in Africa is highly important for identifying locations where the grass supply and demand are in conflict and for the formulation of reasonable and feasible implementation programs for sustainable livestock management.

In this work, we utilize African grassland classification data (Fig. 1) provided by the Aerospace Information Research Institute of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (AIRCAS), combined with MODIS NPP data, meteorological data, topographic data, and livestock statistics from 2001 to 2020, to study the grassland carrying capacity and its status in Africa. Additional details regarding the African grassland classification data are provided in the supplementary information (Supplementary Note 1). This study integrates the current status and trends of grassland carrying capacity to construct a grassland carrying capacity alert index (GCCAI) applicable to diverse data types, including statistical and raster data. The index is then used to quantitatively assess the risk associated with grassland use. The objectives were as follows: (1) to analyze the spatial pattern of the grassland carrying capacity (GCC) and its changing characteristics in Africa from 2001 to 2020; (2) to analyze the status of the grassland carrying capacity in Africa in combination with the livestock and, on this basis, to construct the GCCAI to assess the risk faced by African countries associated with grassland use; (3) to explore the factors influencing grassland carrying capacity in African countries over a period of 20 years and to enhance the understanding of grassland utilization so as to provide theoretical support for the sustainable management of livestock and the livelihoods of pastoralists in African countries.

# Results
Aboveground biomass and grassland carrying capacity
From 2001 to 2020, the average AGB of grasslands in Africa was 196.3 g/m2, as shown in Fig. 2a. Except the Sahara Desert in northern Africa and the area surrounding the Kalahari Desert in southern Africa, all other regions have relatively high multiyear average AGB values. The annual mean AGB of grasslands in Africa showed an increasing trend over a 20-year period, with an annual growth rate of 1.45 g/m2. The trend of the AGB in Africa is shown in Fig. 2b, with 20.3% of the regions exhibiting significant increases, primarily concentrated north of the Azande Plateau, as well as in the Darfur Plateau, the Kordofan Plateau, the Upper Nile Basin, and the southern part of the Congo Basin. The area of increase covers countries such as the DRC, Zambia, South Sudan, Sudan, Ethiopia, Central Africa, Cameroon, and Nigeria. Another 5.8% of the area shows a significant decrease, mainly in the western part of Madagascar and on the east coast of Africa. This area covers Madagascar, South Africa, Mozambique, Tanzania, Kenya, Somalia, and other countries. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows the complete statistical results.
In 2020, the grass supply capacity of countries in eastern and southern Africa was much greater than that of other regions (Fig. 2c - d). Among them, 7 countries had a GCCmin of more than 18 × 106 TLU in 2020, and Angolan GCCmin reached 33.23 × 106 TLU in 2020. These countries account for 36.16% of African total grassland area. The GCC of 18 countries in West Africa and North Africa was less than 2 × 106 TLU in 2020, the smallest GCC was observed for Djibouti (0.02 × 106 TLU) in 2020, and the grassland area of these countries accounted for only 8.37% of the total grassland area of Africa. Among the 49 African countries included in the statistics, 30 countries presented increasing trends in the GCC, with average annual growth rates ranging from 74.09 TLU/yr in Lesotho to 0.62 × 106 TLU/yr in South Sudan (Fig. 2e, f). Over the 20-year period, African overall GCC showed an increasing trend, with an average annual growth rate of 3.43 × 106 TLU/yr for GCCmax and 2.06 × 106 TLU/yr for GCCmin (Fig. 2g). Supplementary Table 1 shows the complete statistical results. We found that substantial decreases in African GCCI occurred in 2002, 2015, and 2019 compared with the previous year and were associated with the impacts generated by torrential rains and floods, extreme droughts, tropical cyclones, and other climate extremes27,28,29. At the national scale, countries south of the Sahara Desert exhibited greater GCC variations compared to those in other regions, indicating that grassland ecosystems in this area are more vulnerable and susceptible to disturbances.

Livestock and grassland carrying status index
According to livestock stock data, livestock in Africa is dominated by seven species (donkeys, camels, cattle, goats, horses, sheep, and mules). Statistical data (Fig. 3a–c) show that major livestock populations have generally maintained a stable growth trend since 2001, with a linear regression fitting equation R2 of 0.99543 and an average annual growth rate of 10.43 × 106 TLU/yr. African total livestock stock in 2020 was 529.19 × 106 TLU, which was 1.59 times greater than that in 2001. African countries have low variability in livestock populations and stable livestock development. In 2020, ten countries in Africa had livestock stocks greater than 18 × 106 TLU. These countries have livestock stocks of 18.94 × 106 TLU ~79.96 × 106 TLU, accounting for 69.28% of the total livestock in African countries, with an annual rate of change of −0.04 × 106 TLU ~2.17 × 106 TLU. Somalia is the only country among these ten where the number of livestock is continuously decreasing. Supplementary Table 2 shows the complete statistical results. The ten countries with the highest livestock inventories did not coincide with those exhibiting the largest GCC values, directly contributing to differential pressures on grassland resource utilization. This observation was confirmed by GCSI assessment results.
As shown in Fig. 3d–h, African GCSI increased significantly from 2001 to 2020, with an average annual increase of 0.015–0.025. The GCSI in 2020 was between 0.96 and 1.60, with the GCSImin exceeding 1 in 2015 and 2019. Supplementary Table 3 shows the complete statistical results. If the growth trend is not effectively controlled, there will not be enough space in the future for African grasslands to support more livestock. However, the utilization of grassland pasture in individual countries shows obvious differences, with the GCSI values of northern African countries being much higher than those of southern African countries. In 17 countries with a GCSImin greater than 2, the local demand for food was not met by the natural grassland alone, and large amounts of external feed were needed to supplement the gap in the pasture supply. In seven countries where the GCSImax was less than 0.2, the demand for livestock food was much lower than the supply capacity of the grassland, and the grassland was underutilized. We found that the variability in the GCSI was significantly greater in countries with higher GCSI values than in countries with low GCSI values from 2001 to 2020. Moreover, the variability in the GCC in countries with high GCSI values was generally high, whereas the change in the number of livestock was stable, suggesting that the GCC of countries with high GCSI values was more susceptible to the change in grassland biomass and that the fluctuation in grassland biomass was the main factor determining the GCSI.

Sustainability assessment of grassland carrying capacity
The results of the African grassland carrying alert for 2020 are shown in Fig. 4. With a minimum GCCAI value of 0.98 and a maximum GCCAI value of 1.62 for the continent, African grassland carrying capacity has been maintained in a healthy state, indicating normal ecological conditions. However, at the national scale, the distribution of grassland resources in Africa exhibits significant disparities, with notable differences observed between northern and southern regions. Among the forty-nine countries in Africa, the alert levels of twenty-five countries indicate serious overexploitation, accounting for 51.02% of the countries in Africa, all of which are located in northern Africa. Countries in northern Africa are at risk of serious overexploitation, whereas countries in southern Africa maintain extremely low utilization of grassland resources. Thirteen countries with extremely low utilization alert levels are located in southern Africa, accounting for 26.53% of all the countries. Countries with minimal or no alert are mainly located in the central region of Africa. For overloaded countries, the risk of overloading is generally high, and there are no countries with a lower risk of overloading. The alert levels of three countries (6.12%), Central Africa, Sierra Leone, and Côte d’Ivoire, indicated extremely low utilization rates. In eight countries (16.33%), Cameroon, Ghana, Guinea, Liberia, Malawi, Swaziland, Togo, Tanzania, there was no alert, indicating that the grassland resources were used at a relatively healthy level.
Analysis of influencing factors
In this study, we characterize the temporal sequence of GCCAI through the Interannual variation of GCSI, focusing on the effect of the Interannual variation of GCSI on the response of each influence factor, and thus analyze the relationship between each influence factor and the results of GCCAI assessment. This study analyzed the effects of climate factors (mean temperature, maximum temperature, minimum temperature, precipitation, Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD), Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI)) and livestock populations on the GCSI across African countries. Climate factors showed different trends from 2001 to 2020. Annual mean temperature (T), annual maximum temperature (Tmax), and Vapor Pressure Deficit (VPD) show significant increasing trends in Africa. The annual minimum temperature (Tmin) and annual cumulative precipitation (PRCP) showed non-significant increasing trends. Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI) showed a non-significant decreasing trend (Supplementary Table 4). Pearson correlation analysis showed that African grassland GCSI was associated with T (r = 0.725, p < 0.01), Tmax (r = 0.606, p < 0.01), PDSI (r = −0.572, p < 0.01), VPD (r = 0.797, p < 0.01), livestock populations ((r = 0.851, p < 0.01)) showed highly significant correlation and non-significant correlation with Tmin (r = 0.235, p = 0.319) and P (r = 0.136, p = 0.567). Supplementary Table 5 shows the complete statistical results.

The relationship between GCSI and the drivers across African countries was further analyzed using multiple linear regression models, and the standardized regression coefficients (Beta) were used to indicate the extent to which each of the drivers influenced changes in GCSI. The results that passed the confidence test are shown in Fig. 5. The supplementary materials include a statistical table presenting the multiple linear regression results (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7). The results showed that the combined contribution of climatic factors and livestock population to GCSI in Africa was 80.4%, and the increase in livestock population was the main cause of the increase in GCSI in Africa. While the increase in PDSI inhibited the extent of GCSI increase. The results of the simulation model varied from country to country. Northern and Eastern African countries exhibited stronger responses to the influencing factors. The combined contribution of climate factors and livestock populations to GCSI ranged from 20.0% to 99.1%. Of the 49 major African countries counted, 30 (61.22%) had livestock populations as the main driver. Of these, 16 were severely overloaded alert countries, 6 very underutilized alert countries, 5 normal countries, and 3 underutilized alert countries. For the severely overloaded and very low utilization alert countries, these countries did not show higher sensitivity to changes in livestock numbers due to very high or very low grass utilization efficiency. This implies that grassland carrying conditions in most African countries are dominated by livestock numbers, and that differences in the degree of livestock development do not enhance the driving effect of livestock numbers on grassland carrying conditions.

# Discussion
Grassland aboveground biomass
Accurate estimation of aboveground biomass (AGB) serves as a fundamental basis for assessing grassland carrying capacity sustainability. The MODIS NPP data product employed in this study has been widely applied in biomass estimation, ecological environment monitoring, and global change research30,31,32. In this study, we estimated the aboveground biomass of African grasslands by integrating MODIS NPP data with canopy cover and topographic data. The results indicate that the AGB of African grasslands exhibited significant spatial heterogeneity. A significant increase in AGB over the 20-year period was observed in east central African countries, along with high grassland AGB. This result is similar to that reported by other studies of grassland AGB in African countries. For example, field measurements by Cox and Waithaka in the Kenyan savanna reported grassland biomass exceeding 1000 g m2 yr−1 33,while our estimates show peak annual cumulative AGB in Kenya ranging from 847.29 to 1140.61 g m−2 yr-1 over a 20-year period, demonstrating strong agreement with field observations. Azin et al. estimated grassland NPP in Tanzania from 2009 to 2019 based on the Monteith method and reported that Tanzanian grasslands experienced high productivity of 7.39 gc m−2 day−1 in 201834. Liu et al. investigated the climate response and spatial and temporal changes in the NPP of grass globally and reported that the NPP of grass on the Ethiopian Plateau, Katanga Plateau, and Azande Plateau exceeded 1000 gc m−2 yr−1 35. A large grass AGB effectively increases the livestock grassland carrying capacity in a region. In this study, the focus was on the AGB that is edible for herbivores, and the use of the terrain slope factor as well as the canopy cover biomass further reduced the herbivore-available grassland AGB; therefore, so our AGB estimates may be lower than those of other scholars. In estimating the aboveground biomass of African grasslands, we masked the African grassland classification data using cropland data from the 30-m resolution GLOBAL Landcover 30 dataset developed by the Institute of Aerospace Information of the Chinese Academy of Sciences36. This method was used to adjust for inaccurate identification of the transition zone between cropland and grassland in the African grassland classification data, and to distinguish between grazing and agricultural land as accurately as possible. Due to the prevalence of agro-pastoral systems and the complexity of the agro-pastoral intertwining zones in Africa, it may still be difficult with the existing data resolution and methods to fully differentiate between agricultural land and grassland and savannah transition areas37. This categorization uncertainty may be more pronounced, especially in areas where small-scale farmland is widely distributed38. Matching of more precise data is needed in the future to accomplish a more accurate land cover classification.

Livestock usable grassland aboveground biomass
Grassland usable rate is a key variable in determining available grassland forage, and rational use of grassland resources can maintain the sustainability of grassland animal husbandry. Factors such as season, water resources, elevation, and trampling by livestock all affect the available pasture. For example, moisture gradients influence the proportion of grassland biomass available for livestock utilization. High-precipitation areas may lead to grass lignification or decomposition, while low-precipitation regions may predominantly support drought-resistant plants with lower nutritional value39. C4 grasses, which predominantly grow in savannas, have low nutritional value and are poorly digested by ruminants40. In addition, uncertainty in precipitation can lead to seasonal and inter-annual fluctuations in vegetation, further limiting the availability of grassland biomass for livestock8. Seasonal livestock migration between wet and dry season pastures, fodder shortages during long dry seasons, and strong interannual variability in biomass production in arid and semiarid areas all exacerbate grazing pressure on local grasslands39,41. In addition, animal diseases, poisonous plants, and often scarce water resources limit grazing possibilities in savannas2,42. Moreover, different grassland types and different study areas can lead to different rates of grass utilization, ranging from 30% in Niger43 and 30% in Ethiopia44 to 45% in Tsavo, eastern Kenya45, and 50% in Tanzania46. There is significant spatial variation in the utilization of grass by livestock, and the use of a single grass utilization rate for estimating the grassland carrying capacity over large areas is inappropriate. However, few relevant thematic studies exist, which makes the assignment of grass utilization rates by region as well as by grass type unfeasible. Therefore, in this study, we used ecological thresholds of lowest value (30%) and the highest value (50%) values to determine grassland utilization in Africa with the premise of maintaining the sustainability of grassland husbandry in Africa, and estimated the range of the GCC in the form of intervals. This method may be more appropriate than others for estimating GCC on the African continent because it takes into account the effects of season, precipitation gradients, livestock trampling, competition from other animals, and the sustainability of grassland resource utilization. Notably, for areas where grassland resources are relatively scarce, slight changes in grassland utilization can lead to large fluctuations in the local GCC, especially in the countries surrounding the Sahel. The actual utilization of grasslands within specific regions needs to be studied further.

Sources of grazing pressure on grasslands
Grassland degradation has been widely acknowledged as a pervasive and severe environmental crisis across Africa. The overexploitation of grasslands through intensive grazing practices triggers profound ecological shifts, including biodiversity loss and productivity decline47,48. Quantifying the impacts of livestock grazing on grasslands is critical to maintaining the health of grassland ecosystems and the sustainability of grassland livestock farming. The countries that received the severe overloading alert generally had a high level of livestock development, and the relative scarcity of grassland resources placed a strong pressure on the grasslands to support livestock; thus, reducing stocking rates is crucial for the sustainability of grasslands countries with scarce resources. However, the decline in livestock numbers has a direct effect on the livelihoods and economies of pastoralists. Involves time-controlled rotations of high-density intensive grazing (rotational grazing) is a method to minimize grazing impacts and improve pasture quality without reducing stocking rates49. Research by Crawford et al. indicates that in East Africa, rotational grazing at appropriate stocking densities can mitigate pasture degradation and enhance the sustainability of savanna ecosystems50. However, findings from Chonco et al. demonstrate that in the Pakkies area of South Africa, rotational grazing does not yield better results for livestock production compared to continuous grazing51. Research is needed to identify effective measures to maintain the balance between livestock management and environmental sustainability, such as exploring mechanisms for livestock industry cooperation between North and South Africa, implementing appropriate rotational grazing strategies, increased export of high-value-added products from animals, sustainable intensification of land use, and supplementation of external feeds17,23,50,52.

The grassland carrying capacity alert index quantifies the threats to the sustainability of grassland pastoralism in African countries, and further exploration of the drivers of these threats contributes to a comprehensive understanding of the changing character of grassland ecosystems. Under natural conditions, the transition zone from humid to arid regions fosters a series of ecosystems adapted to varying levels of precipitation. These ecosystems achieve a state of dynamic equilibrium under long-term stable climatic conditions53. However, climate change (such as shifts in precipitation patterns due to global warming) and human activities (such as overgrazing and deforestation) can disrupt this equilibrium54,55.Recent studies have offered diverse perspectives on the impacts of climatic and anthropogenic factors on vegetation productivity in Africa. Liu et al. discovered that in arid and semi-arid regions with hot, dry climates, there was a significant positive correlation between grassland net primary productivity (NPP) and precipitation35. They also found that the relationship between grassland NPP and mean annual temperature and annual precipitation varied with annual precipitation levels, and that grassland NPP was more sensitive to precipitation than to temperature. Pan et al. concluded that interannual variability in NPP across Africa is primarily driven by continental-scale precipitation rather than temperature fluctuations56. They noted that precipitation significantly influenced terrestrial NPP from 1980 to 2009, accounting for 74% of the interannual variability in NPP. However, extreme increases in precipitation could lead to forest encroachment on grasslands across Africa. Simultaneously, reductions in herbivory could positively affect forest tree cover, while an increase in herbivores could reduce the competitive intensity of grasslands1,57. Tong et al. found that climate change played a dominant role in changes in aboveground net primary productivity (ANPP) in open scrublands, closed scrublands, and non-forested grasslands, while imbalances in hydrothermal conditions and overgrazing led to a decline in ANPP58. Miehe et al., through a 27-year field study in Senegal, observed that fluctuations in precipitation caused variations in herbaceous plant production, while grazing intensity had long-term effects59. The findings of this study, regarding the equilibrium between grassland supply and consumption, indicate that the factors influencing grassland carrying capacity vary among countries. Overall, the increase in livestock population size plays a dominant role in the variation of the grassland carrying status index (GCSI) in Africa. Additionally, the Palmer Drought Severity Index (PDSI) showed a significant negative correlation with the GCSI in Africa, suggesting that increased wetness could alleviate the pressure on African grasslands to support livestock.

Pixel scale GCCAI assessment
This study utilized livestock data sourced from the FAO Statistical Database (FAOSTAT). Due to data availability constraints, the analysis was conducted only at the national level, but the proposed grassland carrying capacity sustainability assessment method is equally applicable to smaller-scale evaluations. Based on the Gridded Livestock of the World (GLW) dataset published by the FAO, we simulated the GCCAI assessment at the pixel level (Fig. 6a). It should be noted that this gridded dataset includes only three years of data (2010, 2015, and 2020), with limited data volume and a relatively large temporal span. For the GCCAI assessment method, which requires continuous long-term time-series inputs, the restricted data availability may lead to overestimation or underestimation of grassland livestock-carrying trends, thereby increasing uncertainty in the assessment results.
For comparison, we generated national-scale GCCAI results using the same years of aggregated national statistical data. The findings indicate that at the pixel level, most regions in countries surrounding the Sahara Desert, as well as parts of eastern African nations such as Kenya, Uganda, South Sudan, Somalia, and Ethiopia, were assessed as Serious overexploitation. In contrast, most areas in Central and Southern African countries exhibited lower sustainability risks. Spatially, the pixel-level and national-scale assessment results showed high consistency, but the pixel-level evaluation better revealed the spatial heterogeneity of grassland carrying capacity sustainability risks within countries.

Our pixel-based assessment results were systematically compared with findings from Piipponen et al. and Rahimi et al.17,21. The results demonstrate that while minor discrepancies exist in risk intensity assessments, there is strong consensus regarding the spatial distribution patterns of grazing pressure across Africa. Specifically, Piipponen et al. confirmed exceptionally high grazing pressure in the Sahel region, while most grassland resources in southern Africa remain relatively underutilized. Similarly, Rahimi et al. demonstrated that in Burkina Faso during 2010−2020, livestock energy demand exceeded forage production in most regions, with the energy supply-demand gap progressively widening—indicating severe sustainability risks for local grasslands. However, discrepancies exist between our results and those of Meshesha et al.22. Through field observations, Meshesha et al. found that the grazing rate in Ethiopia Harshin District far exceeded the local grassland carrying capacity, whereas our pixel-level simulation suggested that grassland utilization in this region remained at a relatively low level. Meshesha et al. reported a mean grassland carrying capacity of 0.3 TLU/ha for this area, which falls within our estimated range of 0.1871–0.3118 TLU/ha. However, significant differences exist in the livestock data used: the GLW dataset lacks information on camels, donkeys, and mules in this region, resulting in our estimated grazing rate of 0.2618 TLU/ha—much lower than Meshesha et al. 5.4 TLU/ha. This discrepancy directly led to divergent assessment outcomes.

This difference highlights that the accuracy of livestock data directly affects grassland carrying capacity sustainability assessments. Access to more precise pixel-level livestock data would significantly improve the reliability of evaluation results. Therefore, future research should focus on developing machine learning-based models that integrate population density, GDP, and water source proximity to build more accurate spatial distribution models of livestock. This approach will improve both the temporal coverage and livestock category completeness of gridded livestock data, ultimately enabling more reliable pixel-scale assessments of grassland carrying capacity sustainability.

Additionally, it should be noted that while this study encompasses all grass species, livestock exhibit selective grazing behavior in practice, meaning certain species may be consumed less due to poor palatability. Therefore, accurately identifying non-edible grassland areas is crucial for improving the precision of grassland carrying capacity assessments. Such refinements would facilitate more accurate small-scale evaluations, providing a more scientific foundation for the sustainable development of animal husbandry.

# Materials and methods
Grassland carrying capacity
Grassland carrying capacity (GCC) indicates the number of animal units that can be sustainably supported by the cumulative grassland biomass per unit area in a year, provided it is sustainable. Not all grassland biomass is available to livestock for reasons of pasture loss, decay, animal trampling, utilization by other organisms, and avoidance of grassland degradation from overuse. The appropriate utilization rate indicates the maximum proportion of grassland biomass that can be used by livestock under this premise18,43,44,45,46. Grassland utilization in Africa is not clearly defined, and the appropriate utilization of grassland varies according to different research purposes and study areas. Grass utilization in Africa clearly varies from a minimum value of 30% in Niger43 to a maximum value of 50% in Tanzania46. Moreover, it is not reasonable to use a single utilization rate for large-scale grassland carrying capacity estimation. To improve the accuracy of the estimation results of the grassland carrying capacity in Africa, in this study, the thresholds of the grass utilization rate were assumed to be the maximum (50%) and minimum (30%) pasture utilization rates identified in the existing studies in Africa. On the basis of the original GCC algorithm60, the concept of the Tropical Livestock Unit (TLU) was used to estimate the grassland carrying capacity range in Africa. The TLU represents a 250 kg live animal with a daily intake of 2.5% of its body weight22. Grassland carrying capacity alert index
The GCSI can reflect the utilization of grassland aboveground biomass (AGB) by livestock in a region over the course of a year. However, a single GCSI can only reflect the grass carrying status at a certain moment in time, and for long-term assessment, multiple GCSI values need to be combined into a time series; this approach is not suitable for characterizing the trends in long time series data. In this study, on the basis of the GCSI, we constructed the GCCAI, which uses an incremental ratio to measure the degree of change in the grassland carrying capacity across a time series. Stability analysis
The coefficient of variation (CV) and linear regression trend were used to assess the changes in the supply and demand of forage over the last 20 years. The CV is the ratio of the standard deviation to the mean, which indicates the degree of dispersion of a dataset and can be used to compare the degree of dispersion and stability of data with different dimensions or different means69,70. The larger the CV is, the greater the degree of dispersion, i.e., the volatility of the data, and the greater the volatility of the data. In this work, the CVs of the GCC, livestock population size, and GCSI were calculated from 2001 to 2020 to reflect their variability over 20 years. Although different grassland types may exhibit significantly different patterns of productivity dynamics within the growing season, biomass accumulation at the interannual scale can mask these short-term dynamic differences triggered by spatial heterogeneity71,72,73.
