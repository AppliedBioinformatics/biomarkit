# Introduction
Due to the influence of human activities, the process of lake eutrophication has been greatly accelerated. Nowadays, lake eutrophication has become a global environment issue [1]. Application of aquatic plants to remediate eutrophic water bodies and rebuild the vitality of aquatic ecosystems is a green and environmentally friendly ecological remediation method [2, 3]. Submerged plants are rooted in sediment of lakes and occupy most of the sediment–water interface. In addition, as the primary producer of aquatic ecosystems, they play an important role in maintaining the functional structure and nutrient cycle of aquatic ecosystems [4]. They absorb nutrients and other substances from water bodies during their growth, and inhibit algae growth by secreting algal suppressants [5]. Their roots can fix sediment to inhibit the resuspension of pollutants in sediment by changing the redox potential between roots. Thus, submerged plants play a crucial role in remediation of eutrophic water bodies [6].

Littering, decline and death are the necessary life stages of submerged plants. During the decomposition process of submerged plants residues, the contained nutrients are released into water bodies under the effects of physical leaching, microbial degradation, and mechanical crushing [7, 8]. Previous studies mainly focused on the effects of decomposition process on the physical and chemical properties of water quality, and the migration of nutrient elements between water body and sediment. The influencing factors of decomposition processes, such as environmental factors of overlying water, environment and composition of sediment, and the role of microorganisms, were also studied [9,10,11,12]. Among them, composition of sediment is a crucial influencing factor [13]. Various pollutants released from the decomposition of submerged plants migrate to the bottom sediment via various pathways, such as precipitation, adsorption, and biological fixation. In addition, the rich nutrients (nitrogen and phosphorus) in sediment have a significant impact on the abundance and activity of microorganisms in the entire aquatic ecosystem [14].

For the whole growing ecosystem of submerged plants, decomposed submerged plants residues in water are an important source of nutrients, while sediment is both a “sink” for absorbing nutrients and a “source” for releasing nutrients [15]. Sediment can store some nutrients released from the decomposition of plant residues by adsorption and sedimentation, playing as a sink of nutrients. While it becomes a source of water pollution when a large amount of plant debris accumulated at the bottom of a lake release some nutrients under microbial decomposition or wind and wave disturbance [9]. Some studies have shown that retaining an appropriate amount of plant residues could provide carbon sources for microorganisms to ensure the denitrification process [16]. Therefore, sediment is key component of aquatic ecosystems and plays a crucial role in the substance cycle [17]. It may generate an important influence on the decomposition rate of submerged plants residues.

This paper aimed to study the effects of different substance compositions on the decomposition process of V. spinulosa Yan, which can provide a theoretical basis for the management of submerged plants in water restoration. The aims are: (1) to study the effects of different substrate composition on the migration and transformation of nitrogen and phosphorus in the growing ecosystem of V. spinulosa Yan; (2) to analyze the effects of different substrate composition on the releasing characteristics of DOM (soluble organic matter) during the decomposition of V. spinulosa Yan; (3) to explore the effects of different substrate composition on microbial communities and diversity in the ecosystem; and (4) to discuss the potential relationship between the microorganisms in the system and the substance transformation in the decomposition process of V. spinulosa Yan.

# Materials and methods
Plant materials
Fresh V. spinulosa Yan was collected from Mingxin Lake in Wenzhou University (Wenzhou, China), and the TN and TP of the Lake water were 3.31 and 0.17 mg/L, respectively. After the sediment and impurities on the plant roots were washed with tap water, the whole plants were washed with ultrapure water, air-dried and oven dried at 65 ℃ to constant weight. The dried plant residues were cut into about 1 cm and put into a 100 mesh nylon mesh bag for later use. The ceramic and iron–carbon fillers used in the experiment were purchased commercially.

Preparation of sediment and additives
The sediment used in the experiment were the surface sediment collected from the same lake in Wenzhou University in June 2022. After 1–3 days of sedimentation, the supernatant was removed, and the bottom sediment was transferred to the flat plastic film for air-drying. After removing the impurities, it was ground by a grinder and sieved (100 mesh). After fully mixing, it was placed on the bottom of the experimental device. The relevant physical and chemical properties of the sediment, ceramic and iron–carbon fillers used in the decomposition experiment are shown in Table S1.

Experimental design
The decomposition experiment was conducted in a constant temperature incubator from July 2022, and the temperature was set at 17 ± 2 ℃. The sediment after screening was mixed and put on the bottom in polypropylene plastic beakers (top diameter × bottom diameter × height = 21.9 × 19.6 × 26.4 cm), which were first washed with 5% dilute HCl solution and washed with distilled water. The layer height of the sediment in each plastic beaker was 10 cm, and the dry weight was of the sediment 800 ± 5 g. Four experimental groups with addition of the plant residues were labeled as D, L, T, and G, representing the sediment group, sediment + ceramic group (7:3, v/v), sediment + iron–carbon group (7:3, v/v), and sediment + calcium nitrate group (141 g/m2) [18, 19]. The sediment in the corresponding control groups without addition of the plant residues was the same as those of the experimental groups. Each experimental and control group were set up in three parallels. Then, 4.5 L of distilled water after exposure for 3 days was slowly injected in each plastic beaker. After the sediment was settled for 3 days, the initial matrix samples of each group were collected and recorded as day 0. Subsequently, a nylon mesh bag containing 10 g of V. spinulosa Yan litters was completely immersed in the water and put on the surface of the sediment. The water and sediment samples of each group were collected at 1, 2, 4, 7, 10, 17, 24, 31, 41, and 55 days. The microbial samples in the overlying water, sediment, and plant litters were collected at 7, 24, and 55 days, respectively. At each sampling time, a total of 150 mL of overlying water in the upper, middle and lower parts was collected by siphon method, and the sediment samples were collected by columnar sampler. After natural drying, the sediment samples were ground through a 100-mesh sieve for subsequent analysis. During the experiment, the distilled water after exposure was regularly supplemented to keep the constant water volume.

Analytical methods
Determination of water and sediment samples
Dissolved oxygen (DO), pH, and oxidation–reduction potential (ORP) in the overlying water were measured by YSI ProQuatro portable multi-parameter water quality analyzer. The concentrations of TN, TP, phosphate (PO43−–P), ammonia nitrogen (NH4+–N), nitrate nitrogen (NO3−–N) and nitrite nitrogen (NO2−–N) in the overlying water were determined according to the national standard. The contents of TN, NH4+–N and NO3−–N in sediment were measured in terms of the semimicro-Kjeldahl method. TP, inorganic phosphorus (IP), organic phosphorus (OP), NaOH-extracted phosphorus (NaOH–P) and HCl-extracted phosphorus (HCl–P) were determined by the international SMT grading extraction method [20, 21].

Fluorescence analysis of DOM
The three-dimensional fluorescence spectra of the overlying water samples were measured using an ultra-fast three-dimensional excitation emission matrix fluorescence spectrophotometer (3DEEM) (Aqualog, HORIBA Scientific, France). The main parameters of the instrument were: the light source was a 150 W xenon lamp, the PMT voltage was 700 V, and the signal-to-noise ratio was > 110. The measurement parameters were the excitation wavelength (Ex) of 245 ~ 500 nm, the emission wavelength (Em) of 244.24 ~ 826.05 nm, the scanning speed of 2400 nm/min, the integration time of 0.5 s, the step (Increment) of excitation and emission of 5 nm, and the medium CCD gain.

Microbial sampling and analysis
After autoclave sterilization of sampling bottle, filter membrane and suction filter device, the collected water (200–500 mL) was filtered with 0.22 μm filter membrane to obtain visible covering on the filter membrane. When the suction filtration was completed, the water volume of the suction filtration was recorded, and the filter membrane was carefully transferred to a refrigerator and stored at − 80 ℃.

Microbial sampling method in plant residues: After high-pressure steam sterilization of sampling tools, filter membranes, and suction filtration devices, the plant residues were taken out from the mesh bag and weighed. 10 mL of sterilized ultrapure water was added to per gram of the plant residue sample and sonicated for 1 min. After vortexing for 10 s, ultrasonic oscillation was repeated twice. The washing solution from the two ultrasonic oscillation processes was mixed and filtered with 0.22 μm filter membrane to obtain obvious covering, and the filter membrane was carefully transferred to – 80 ℃ refrigerator for preservation.

Finally, the microbial samples were stored in dry ice and transported to Shanghai Meiji Technology Co., Ltd. to carry out subsequent microbial high-throughput sequencing analysis.

Calculation of nitrogen and phosphorus release fluxes at sediment–water interface
The release fluxes of nitrogen and phosphorus in the sediment were calculated by static culture method [22]. The specific operation was: A certain amount of the sediment was taken out and cultured statically in the laboratory. After the overlying water was injected, the water sample was immediately collected as the initial value, then the water sample of the overlying water was collected by siphon method within a certain interval (e.g., 8–12 h once). At the same time, the overlying water was supplemented to maintain the constant water volume in the cultivation device. The whole culture process was lasted for 3 days, and the concentrations of NH4+–N and PO43−–P in the overlying water were measured.

Statistics and analysis
The data obtained from the experiment were analyzed by IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 for analysis of variance. One-way analysis of variance was used between the experimental group and the control group. Three-dimensional fluorescence analysis was completed by MatlabR2022 b with the installation of DOMFluor toolkit. Origin 2019 b was used for drawing.

# Results
Effects of different substrate composition on the migration and transformation of nitrogen and phosphorus
Migration and transformation of nitrogen
Due to the high solubility of calcium nitrate, the concentration of TN in the overlying water of group G remained at a high level within 0–10 days. During the initial stage (0–10 or 17 days), the TN concentration in the overlying water of D, L and T groups exhibited a significant upward trend, indicating that the decomposition of V. spinulosa Yan significantly increased the nitrogen content in the overlying water. The maximum value of TN content in the overlying water of T group was only 5.68 mg/L, which was significantly lower than that of other groups (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1A–D). This may be attributed to the removal of TN by iron–carbon filler. The electrons generated by the iron–carbon filler anode are beneficial to the conversion of NO3−–N and NO2−–N to N2, which promotes the autotrophic denitrification [23]. The concentration of TN in the group G was significantly lower than that in the corresponding control group.

The concentration of TN in the experimental group G was significantly lower than that in the control group, which may be due to the nutrients released by V. spinulosa Yan decay as a carbon source for microorganisms. In this study, the microbial community in group G showed obvious enrichment of aerobic denitrifying bacteria, which promoted heterotrophic denitrification and thus reduced TN in water [24].

In Fig. 1E–H, NH4+–N kept rising trend in the initial stage of decomposition (0–2 or 7 days), which was accompanied by the ammonification of microorganisms. A large amount of organic nitrogen was converted into NH4+–N and further converted into NO3−–N, which was also proved by the increase of NO3−–N in Fig. 1I–L. However, during the rapid leaching period (0–7 days), a large number of soluble substances were leached from the plant residues, providing nutrients for aerobic heterotrophic microorganisms in the water to facilitate their mass reproduction. The decomposition process is an oxygen-consuming process, leading to the rapid decrease of DO in the overlying water (Fig. S1), and the anaerobic condition of the overlying water promoted the denitrification process. Therefore, the concentration of NO3−–N in the overlying water showed a downward trend in 4–10 days. As the DO in the overlying water gradually increased, nitrification became the dominated nitrogen removal process, and the concentration of NH4+–N in the water began to decrease, while the concentration of NO3−–N remained the increasing trend. The concentration of NH4+–N in the overlying water of group L and group T was lower than that of other experimental groups in the early stage of decomposition (0–7 days). This may be due to the large specific surface area of ceramic and iron–carbon fillers, and the loose porous structure has a certain adsorption effect on ammonia nitrogen [25]. The electrons produced by the anode of iron–carbon fillers are beneficial to the conversion of NO3−–N and NO2−–N to N2. In addition, the decomposition of macromolecular organic matters by iron–carbon fillers also provides carbon source for heterotrophic denitrification process and promotes denitrification [26].

Nitrogen is one of the essential elements for primary production of organisms, and also a key nutrient element for nutrient cycling in aquatic ecosystems. Migration and transformation pathways for nitrogen in aquatic ecosystems include assimilation by aquatic plants, nitrification and denitrification by microorganisms, accumulation and volatilization in sediment. During the decomposition of submerged plants, the nitrogen content in the overlying water increases sharply, which breaks the original dynamic balance of adsorption and release between water and sediment, increasing the equilibrium concentration of nitrogen and the possibility of nitrogen re-release in sediment. Figure 2 reveals the changes of TN, NH4+–N and NO3−–N in the sediment during the decomposition experiment. In the early stage, due to the fast decomposition rate of residues and the high levels of nutrients in water, the mineralization of organic nitrogen by ammoniating bacteria in sediments was promoted [27]. Therefore, the TN content in the sediment decreased significantly, which was also demonstrated by the continuous increase of NH4+–N concentration in Fig. 2E–H. In the later stage, the decomposition rate of V. spinulosa Yan reduced, and the mineralization of organic nitrogen in sediment gradually decreased. The nitrogen in the overlying water released from the decomposition of V. spinulosa Yan gradually transferred to the sediment under the effect of the concentration difference of interstitial water, resulting in the gradual increase of TN content in the sediment. The TN in the sediment of L and T groups decreased to a certain extent. The TN content in the ceramics and iron–carbon fillers continued to rise in the initial stage, indicating that the two fillers have good adsorption performance for nitrogen. Subsequently, the concentration of TN in the ceramic gradually decreased, even was lower than the initial value in the later stage. This may be because the ceramic released the adsorbed nitrogen again in the later stage. It may be the reason for that the NH4+–N release flux in the sediment–water interface of the L group at the end of the experiment (Fig. S2) was significantly higher than those of other experimental groups.

The initial concentration and release flux of NH4+–N in the sediment of group G (Fig. S2) were significantly higher than those of other groups. This is mainly due to that a large amount of nitrate added to the sediment will inhibit the nitrification reaction, resulting in the accumulation of NH4+–N in the sediment [28]. It has also been shown that Ca2+ in calcium nitrate can replace NH4+–N from the sediment [29].

Migration and transformation of phosphorus
Figure 3 shows that the phosphorus in the overlying water reached the maximum value at 4 days, which was significantly earlier than the release of nitrogen in the V. spinulosa Yan residues. After 4 days, with the decrease of decomposition rate and the increase of DO, Fe3+ and Al3+ ions in the sediment existed in a higher valence state. Fe3+ and Al3+ not only combined with phosphate in the overlying water to form insoluble iron phosphate, but also significantly reduced the releasing intensity of phosphorus in the sediment [30]. The diffusion flux of DRP (Fig. S3) was negative, also indicating the transforming of phosphorus in the overlying water into the sediment. The initial concentration of TP in the overlying water of the T group was 0.023 mg/L, and the maximum concentration was only 7.05 mg/L, which was lower than that of other groups. It may be due to that the iron–carbon fillers could ionize a large amount of Fe2+, which was oxidized to Fe3+ after the recovery of DO, thus producing adsorption and precipitation of phosphate in the overlying water [31]. Moreover, the Fe(OH)3 precipitation produced by the hydrolysis of Fe3+ also has a certain adsorption and fixation effect on phosphate in water [32]. Therefore, the concentrations of TP and SRP in the overlying water of the T group were significantly lower than those of other experimental groups during the same period.

The initial concentrations of TP, IP, OP and NaOH–P were 646.94, 582.7, 64.23, and 117.21 mg/kg, respectively. Compared with the initial values, the phosphorus content of all forms in the sediment increased by 37.36, 27.42, 9.94, and 13.15 mg/kg, respectively (Fig. S4). This shows that the phosphorus released by the plant residues is eventually transferred to the sediment. According to present studies, the adsorption of phosphorus in water by sediment mainly includes electrostatic adsorption, ion exchange ligand, and surface complexation, among which ion exchange, and electrostatic adsorption [33,34,35]. At the same time, due to the large difference in the composition and binding mode of each form of phosphorus, different reasons are responsible for the increase in the content of different forms of phosphorus.

According to the different solubility of phosphorus in acidic and alkaline solutions, IP in sediment is divided into NaOH–P (or Fe/Al–P) and HCl–P (or Ca–P). Among them, NaOH–P is the phosphorus in the sediment that is easily reduced by iron, aluminum oxide or hydroxide. It is more easily released and has high bioavailability [36]. It can be seen from Fig. 4 that the increase of NaOH–P in the sediment of the T group accounted for 53.81% of the total increase of TP at the end of the experiment, which was significantly higher than that of other groups. Previous studies have shown that the release of phosphorus in sediment is closely related to the redox process of Fe [37]. The iron–carbon fillers added in the sediment of the T group spontaneously formed the primary battery reaction in the water, and the positive electrode of the battery produced a large amount of Fe2+ [38]. With the increase of DO in the overlying water, Fe2+ would be oxidized to Fe3+, and Fe3+ could combine with H2PO4−- and HPO4− in the overlying water to form a stable iron phosphate precipitate. Moreover, due to the pH > 6 in the sediment, Fe2+ and Fe3+ ions showed good flocculation ability, and Fe(OH)2 and Fe(OH)3 generated by hydrolysis further adsorbed phosphorus in the overlying water. In addition, the co-precipitation of Fe3+ and Fe(OH)3 also contributed to the adsorption of active phosphorus in the overlying water [39].

HCl–P, also known as calcium-bound phosphorus in sediment, is usually considered to be phosphorus produced by natural processes. It mainly composes of its own calcium phosphorus and detrital calcium phosphorus. Its own calcium phosphorus includes apatite, sedimentary calcium carbonate, and fluorapatite formed by early diagenesis. Detrital calcium phosphorus is mineralized from animal and plant residues, bones, etc., with low solubility and stable existence form [40]. It is mostly regarded as permanent phosphorus sinks. Figure 4 shows that the increment of HCl–P in the sediment of the L group and G group was significantly higher than that of other groups. At the end of the experiment, the increment of HCl–P in the two groups accounted for 51.20 and 52.40% of the total increment of TP, respectively. Previous studies have shown that calcium nitrate has strong oxidizing properties and can greatly increase the ORP of sediment [18]. It can be seen from Figs. S1 and S5 that during the experiment, the DO and ORP in the overlying water of the G group were significantly higher than those of other groups, which also demonstrated the strong oxidation of calcium nitrate. Therefore, addition of calcium nitrate may promote the transformation of Fe2+ to Fe3+, thus enhancing the adsorption and precipitation of phosphate. At the same time, the DO and ORP in the overlying water of the G group remained at a high level for a long time during the experiment. Thus, the Fe and Al in the sediment can maintain a high valence state, which inhibits the conversion of Fe/Al–P to soluble phosphate [41]. In addition, due to the high solubility of calcium nitrate, a large amount of Ca2+ is released. Therefore, the contact chance of phosphate between the overlying and interstitial water and Ca2+ is greatly increased. Under the interaction of ionic bonds, the rich calcium ions promote the formation of insoluble Ca–P, resulting in the precipitation and adsorption of phosphate into the bottom mud [42]. At the same time, under the condition of ion exchange, a large amount of Ca2+ will replace Fe3+ and Al3+, thus promoting the transformation of Fe/Al–P to more insoluble Ca–P. The significant increase in Ca–P content in the L group may be due to the porous interior of the ceramic. Its large specific surface area is easy for attachment and growth of microorganisms, thus a large number of microbial remains are mineralized to form HCl–P [43].

OP includes refractory organic phosphorus composed of terrigenous emissions and degradable organic phosphorus composed of aquatic corpses [44]. During the experiment, the increment of OP in the sediment of the four groups was only 8.62–9.94 mg/kg (Fig. S4), which was significantly lower than that of other forms of phosphorus. It is speculated that the increase of OP in the sediment is mainly derived from the decomposition of the V. spinulosa Yan residues and the debris of epiphytic microorganisms during the decomposition process.

Effects of different matrix composition on DOM in overlying water
DOM produced during plant and microbial decomposition is the most active component of organic matter. It has a complex structure and is widely distributed. Its existence greatly affects the generation and transformation of pollutants in aquatic systems. For example, the complexation, degradation and condensation humification of DOM with new contaminants such as heavy metals, pharmaceuticals, and personal care products may pose potential risks to water quality of water bodies [45].

Figure 5 reveals the changes of DOM in the overlying water during the decomposition of V. spinulosa Yan in the D group. The fluorescent substances in EEM were not obvious within 2–4 days. At 7–10 days, there were obvious visible tryptophan-like T1 peak and visible tyrosine B1 peak in EEM, as well as weak fulvic acid A peak in ultraviolet region. It may be due to that the soluble part of the V. spinulosa Yan residues quickly leaches into the overlying water in the initial stage of decomposition, and the nutritional level of the overlying water increases. At this stage, the DO in the overlying water slowly increases with more active microorganisms, which promotes the decomposition process to produce the organic matters with small molecular such as tryptophan, tyrosine, and amino acid [46]. At the same time, the microbial community in the overlying water also contains protein-like substances, so the DOM in the overlying water is mostly protein-like. The fluorescence group intensity of humic-like substances in DOM was weak, which may be derived from the product of microbial metabolites [47].

During 10–24 days, the contents of protein-like and humus-like substances in DOM continued to increase. It is speculated that the refractory substances in the V. spinulosa Yan residues are gradually decomposed and released into the overlying water under the action of microorganisms and enzymes [48]. In addition, the contents of fulvic and humic acid-like substances in DOM were increased.

In 31–44 days, the fluorescence intensity of the protein-like peak and the humic-like peak increased significantly. It indicates that in the middle and late stages of the decomposition of V. spinulosa Yan residues, a large amount of protein-like and humus-like substances are released into the overlying water. This is due to that the insoluble components in the plant residues are further decomposed under the action of microorganisms, and the microbial metabolic process produces humic-like substances. Therefore, the intensity of fluorescence group of humic-like acids gradually increased. Besides the continuous release of protein-like substances during the decomposition of the plant residues, the protein-like substances contained in the debris of dead microorganisms in the overlying water also contribute to the increase of tryptophan, tyrosine, and other components [49].

In the late stage of decomposition (55 days), the fluorescence intensity of the protein-like peak in the fluorescence spectrum of the overlying water DOM decreased significantly, but the UV fulvic A peak and the humic-like M peak still maintained high fluorescence intensity. DOM in sediments is related to leaching of terrigenous DOM and humic acid from soil. These terrestrial humic-like substances affected the fluorescence intensity distribution of peak A [50]. It shows that the main DOM in the overlying water is humic-like substances at this time. This is due to the large consumption of protein-like substances in the overlying water by microorganisms [51]. Therefore, the intensity of the fluorescent groups related to protein-like substances in the picture gradually decreased. The macromolecular substances that are not easily degraded in the V. spinulosa Yan residues, such as the complex formed by the interweaving of cellulose and lignin connected by various covalent bonds in the plant cell wall, will not only hinder the decomposition of DOM in the overlying water by microorganisms, but also the humic-like substances produced by the decomposition process will maintain strong fluorescence intensity for a long time [50, 52].

Based on the three-dimensional fluorescence spectrum of DOM in the overlying water of the experimental groups, different matrix compositions did not significantly affect the composition of the organic matter released during the decomposition of V. spinulosa Yan. During the experiment, the similar change trend of DOM in the overlying water of the four experimental groups was observed. Protein-like B1 and T1 peaks and weak ultraviolet fulvic acid A peaks appeared first in the early stage of the decomposition process, indicating that the main DOM released in the early stage is protein. With the proceeding of the decomposition, the insoluble organic matter in V. spinulosa Yan is decomposed into fulvic acid and humus under the action of microorganisms. This process also consumes a large amount of the substances with small molecular such as proteins and amino acids in the overlying water. Therefore, in the middle stage of decomposition, the proportion of protein-like substances in the DOM decrease, and the proportion of fulvic acid-like and humic-like substances increase significantly. At the end of the decomposition, the refractory components in V. spinulosa Yan are largely decomposed. The humic-like M peak and humic-like F peak in DOM still maintained strong fluorescence intensity, while the fluorescence intensity of protein-like B1 and T1 peaks decreased further. However, the time and intensity of the fluorescence peaks in each experimental group were different. The three-dimensional fluorescence spectra of the L group (Fig. S6) showed protein-like B1 and T1 peaks from the second day. The G group (Fig. S8) showed obvious fluorescent substances on the 10 days, and its fluorescence intensity was slightly lower than that of other three experimental groups at the same time. This may be due to that the loose and porous ceramic is suitable for microbial attachment and growth, so the activity of microorganisms in the L group is stronger in the initial stage of the decomposition. However, the higher concentration of NO2−–N in the overlying water of the G group would have a toxic effect on the microorganisms and reduce their activity (Fig. 1).

Effects of different substrate composition on microorganisms in the process of decomposition
Microorganisms play an important role in the decomposition process of submerged plant residues, especially in the degradation and fragmentation stage. The presence of microorganisms affects the whole decomposition process of submerged plants. Microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, and protozoa are important decomposers for plant residues.

To explore the changes and differences of microbial community composition in each experimental group during the decomposition of V. spinulosa Yan, Alpha diversity analysis was performed on 36 microbial samples in the overlying water, sediment, and plant residues. Analysis of six indexes (sobs, Shannon, Simpson, ace, chao1, and coverage) at OUT level were conducted in Table 1. Sobs, Chao 1 and Ace reflect the community richness index, and the higher the values of Chao 1 and Ace correspond to the higher the community species richness (species). Shannon and Simpson reflect community diversity index. The smaller Simpson value indicates the higher community diversity. Coverage reflects community coverage index, Good"s species coverage [53]. The population characteristics and abundance of microbial samples in the overlying water, sediment and residue in the different decomposition stages are different with different matrix components. The overlying water, sediment and plant residues have specific microorganisms. The number of specific microorganisms in sediment is 752, which is significantly higher than that of the overlying water (76) and plant residues (57). Among the four different substrates, the G group has the largest number of specific OTUs (182).

To further analyze the changing process of microbial community in different groups, the composition of dominant phylum of epiphytic microorganisms with abundance greater than 1% in D, L, T, and G were analyzed on 7 days, 24 days, and 55 days (Fig. 6).

Among the microbial samples in the L group, Firmicutes (41.5%) is the most abundant phylum, followed by Proteobacteria (38.5%), Bacteroidota (12.3%), Actinobacteriota (4.1%), and Desulfobacterota (1.7%). At 7 days, the proportion of Proteobacteria in the overlying water of the L group is significantly higher than that of other groups, and the proportion of Firmicutes and Bacteroidota in the sediment increased gradually with the proceeding of the decomposition process. The initial proportion of Firmicutes in the epiphytic microbial community of the plant residue is significantly higher than that of other groups, and reach the maximum abundance at 55 days. In the stage of rapid leaching of V. spinulosa Yan residues (Fig. S9), Pseudomonadales in the L group accounts for a high proportion. Pseudomonadales belongs to the genus of aerobic denitrifying bacteria under the class of γ-Proteobacteria and is a kind of heterotrophic organisms [54]. It indicates that the L group can supply more organic matter for microbial reproduction and promote the colonization of microorganisms in the overlying water. Similar to Proteobacteria, Bacteroidales, which belongs to Bacteroidota, is a group of microorganisms that play an important role in protein decomposition [55]. The proportion of Bacteroidales in the sediment of the L group gradually increases, indicating the suitable environment for the growth of microorganisms in the sediment. Flavobacteriales under the phylum of Bacteroidota is a kind of obligate anaerobic bacteria, which plays an important role in the degradation of refractory compounds in plants [56]. It can produce lactic acid, succinic acid, and other organic acids by consuming polysaccharides and glucans in plants [57]. The proportion of Flavobacteriales in the epiphytic microbial community of the L group remained a high level in the early and middle stages of the decomposition process, which may be the reason for the appearance of the protein-like substances in the fluorescence spectra of DOM in the overlying water of the L group from the second day.

Among the microbial samples in the T group, Proteobacteria (47.4%) is the most abundant phylum, followed by Firmicutes (40.9%), Bacteroidota (5.3%), Desulfobacterota (2.9%), and Actinobacteriota (2.0%). During the whole decomposition process (Fig. S10), the proportion of Desulfobacterota in the sediment of the T group increases significantly with the proceeding of the decomposition process. Firmicutes in the plant residues of the T group maintains a high abundance. Desulfitobacteriales and Desulfotomaculales under the phylum of Firmicutes can obtain energy by oxidizing organic compounds or reducing molecular hydrogen to hydrogen sulfide. They participate in the decomposition and mineralization of organic matters, which is closely related to the removal and circulation of phosphorus [58, 59]. Comparing with other experimental groups, the microbial communities in each part of the T group have a higher abundance. This may be due to the presence of Fe2+ and Fe3+ in the system to promote the growth of microorganisms. Fe is one of the basic elements required for the growth of many microorganisms. Important biological processes such as electron transfer, photosynthesis, gene regulation, and DNA synthesis need the participation of Fe [60, 61].

Among the microbial samples in the G group, Proteobacteria (61.6%) is the most abundant phylum, followed by Firmicutes (22.4%), Bacteroidota (8.7%), Actinobacteriota (4.5%), and Desulfobacterota (0.2%). The changes in the abundance of microbial community in the overlying water of the G group are significantly lower than that of other three experimental groups (Fig. S11). Due to the high solubility of calcium nitrate, the concentration of NO2−–N in the overlying water of the G group maintains at a high level in the initial 10 days after the addition of calcium nitrate to the sediment (Fig. 1). Excessive NO2−–N will affect the activity of microorganisms and even cause toxic effects [62]. This may be the reason for the significantly lower abundance of microbial communities in the overlying water of the G group was than that of other experimental groups. Meanwhile, the addition of calcium nitrate to the sediment changes the ecological environment of the sediment. The presence of a large amount of NO3−–N and Ca2+ and a high redox potential result in the environment of the sediment for the growth of specific microorganisms, promoting the development of bacterial community to the concentrated direction. For example, a large amount of NO3−–N provides electron acceptors for heterotrophic and facultative anaerobic bacteria in the sediment, promoting the decomposition of organic matters and mass propagation of these bacteria, while inhibiting the growth of other bacteria. Xanthomonadales, which is only detected in the sediment of the G group, belongs to γ-Proteobacteria and can produce spores. Most of them are chemoheterotrophic bacteria, which have the ability to decompose compounds such as sugars and proteins, and have strong resistance to external adverse environments [63]. Azospirillales, which is not detected in the sediment of the G group, is a kind of nitrifying bacterium. Excessive NO3−–N is not conducive to its growth. Desulfobacterota is neither detected in the sediment of the G group. It is speculated that the transformation of phosphorus in the sediment of the G group may be mainly caused by physical and chemical effects, such as replacing of Al3+ and Fe3+ by Ca2+ to generate Ca–P. Therefore, the effect of microorganisms plays a minor role in the transformation of phosphorus.

The microorganisms in the D, L, T, and G groups were grouped, and a heatmap of the microbial community at the genus level was drawn, as shown in Fig. 7. The bacteria of TOP 20 in each sample were shown in the map.

Both Acinetobacter and Flavobacterium belong to the phylum of Bacteroidota. Flavobacterium belongs to strictly aerobic bacteria. Bacteroidota is one of the most abundant bacterial phyla in freshwater waters [64]. Bacillus and Clostridium belong to the phylum of Firmicutes. Bacillus is strictly aerobic and facultative anaerobic bacterium, which absorbs nutrients by means of nitrogen fixation and phosphate dissolution [65, 66]. Clostridium is anaerobic non-symbiotic nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which is related to the hydrolysis process of sugar and protein [67]. Under different substrate conditions, the relative abundance of Acinetobacter, Bacillus, Clostridium, Flavobacterium, and Enterobacter is higher. Enterobacter belongs to the phylum of Proteobacteria. Pseudomonas and Lysobacter belong to the phylum of Proteobacteria, both of which have the highest abundance in the G group.

To classify the function of the dominant genera in different substrates, the FAPRPTAX functional group difference test was performed on the microbial genera with relative abundance TOP10 (Fig. 8). Chemoheterotrophy and aerobic chemoheterotrophy are the two most important functions in the four substrates. Because NO3−–N can provide electron acceptors for heterotrophic denitrifying bacteria, generating aerobic or anaerobic denitrification in the environments with different DO content.

Nitrogen fixation is the biological nitrogen fixation process by nitrogen-fixing bacteria through nitrogenase, which converts N2 in the atmosphere into bioavailable nitrogen [68]. The nitrogen fixation function of the L group is stronger, indicating the stronger ability of biological nitrogen fixation in this group and the higher accumulation of NH4+–N. The ceramic may release the early adsorbed nitrogen again in the later stage of the decomposition process. Therefore, the NH4+–N release flux at the sediment–water interface of the L group is significantly higher than that of other experimental groups at the end of the experiment (Fig. S2). The nitrogen fixation function of the G group is relatively weak, resulting in the weak ability to fix the accumulated NH4+–N in the sediment. Therefore, the NH4+–N flux released in the initial stage of the G group is significantly higher than that of other groups. This is consistent with the conclusion of nitrogen migration and transformation in this study.

Nitrate reduction is a redox reaction in organisms. The nitrate reduction function of the G group is stronger, and the NO3−–N content in the overlying water of the G group is the highest the initial stage (Fig. 1). It is due to the reduction of NO3−–N to NO2−–N by nitrate reduction [69].

Conclusions
This study investigated the effects of growth substrates on the decomposition process of eelgrass. The iron–carbon fillers in sediment reduced the concentration concentrations of TN and TP released from the V. spinulosa Yan residues into the overlying water. It is mainly due to the strong adsorption ability of the iron–carbon fillers. The addition of ceramic and calcium nitrate promoted the formation of HCl–P in the sediment. The addition of iron–carbon filler resulted in an increase in the content of NaOH–P in the sediment. Different substrates conditions have insignificant effect on the compositions of the released organic matter. Due to different type and availability of nutrients in different substrates, significant differences in the microbial community structure were observed. Pseudomonadales, Bacteroidales, and Flavobacteriales, which can decompose macromolecular organic matter, were the dominant bacteria in the L. The T fillers system exhibited significantly higher abundance of microbial community than other systems, with abundant functional bacteria related to denitrification and sulfate reduction. However, a smaller change of microbial community abundance was found with addition of calcium nitrate, with the enrichment of aerobic denitrifying bacteria.
