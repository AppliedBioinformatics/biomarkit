# Introduction
Land degradation, water-induced soil erosion, and declining agricultural productivity are closely interlinked challenges in mountainous ecosystems that are further exacerbated by climate change. These issues pose serious threats to the sustainable development of hill agro-ecosystems (Manandhar et al., 2011). Inappropriate land use practices and inadequate soil and water management have been identified as major drivers of land degradation in such fragile environments (Chaturvedi et al., 2016). Soil and water losses from sloping agricultural lands not only reduce crop productivity but also undermine the ecological integrity and natural functioning of hill ecosystems (Lenka et al., 2012). The Himalayan ecosystem provides a vital resource base, supporting high-value agriculture, extensive forest cover, and perennial river systems that supply drinking water and irrigation, hydropower generation, rich biodiversity, and tourism. The ecological security of this region is therefore critical for sustaining the livelihoods of nearly 1.3 billion people across Asia (Rana, 2025). Within this context, agroforestry has emerged as a promising land use system for enhancing productivity, conserving natural resources, and improving livelihood resilience, particularly for rural communities in hilly regions (Rizvi et al., 2017).

The Shivalik region, located in the northwestern part of India between 28°57′16″ and 34°10′48″ N latitudes and 73°29′24″ and 80°14′23″ E longitudes, extends from Jammu and Kashmir in the northwest to Uttarakhand in the southeast, covering parts of Punjab, Himachal Pradesh, Chandigarh, Haryana, and Uttar Pradesh. This region spans approximately 3.33 million hectares and is characterized by undulating terrain, steep slopes, and highly erodible soils (Yadav et al., 2015). Timely irrigation remains one of the most critical constraints to sustainable development in the region. The dominance of small and marginal farmers with landholdings of less than 1 ha, combined with erratic rainfall, degraded soils, and limited access to resources, further constrains the productivity and sustainability of rainfed agriculture which, in the Shivaliks, is predominantly a maize–wheat cropping system.

Despite receiving sufficient annual rainfall (approximately 1,100 mm), the sloping topography of the Shivalik region results in substantial runoff losses, limiting effective soil moisture availability for crops. During the monsoon season, high runoff volumes coupled with steep slopes generate severe soil erosion, accelerating land degradation. However, this same topography also presents opportunities for water harvesting and resource conservation if managed appropriately. Immediate and site-specific interventions are therefore essential to ensure the long-term sustainability of these fragile sloping lands (Pal et al., 2016).

Agroforestry systems, when strategically designed and implemented along slope positions, offer significant potential for soil and water conservation, enhanced biomass production, and diversified livelihoods. As a core component of eco-village approaches (a holistic model for sustainable living that integrates ecological, social, economic, and cultural dimensions), agroforestry contributes to restoring ecosystem functions and delivering multiple ecosystem services in hilly agro-ecosystems (Jose, 2009; Jinger et al., 2022). Trees integrated within agricultural landscapes sequester atmospheric carbon dioxide in biomass and soils, thereby contributing to climate change mitigation (Nair et al., 2009). In addition, agroforestry supports food and nutritional security, particularly in rainfed and degraded landscapes (Jinger et al., 2023).

However, the performance of agroforestry systems, including tree growth, biomass accumulation, and carbon storage, is strongly influenced by site-specific factors such as slope position, soil moisture availability, nutrient status, and microclimatic conditions (Chen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025). These variations highlight the need for slope-position–based planning and evaluation of agroforestry interventions.

Although agroforestry in India has been extensively studied for its role in livelihood enhancement and carbon sequestration, long-term, terrain-focused assessments remain limited. This gap is particularly pronounced in regions with undulating topography, where slope-driven variability strongly governs ecological processes and system performance. There is a pressing need to generate empirical evidence from local field experiments to improve estimates of biomass production and soil organic carbon storage and to inform land degradation neutrality and climate change mitigation strategies (Pal and Panwar, 2013; Panwar et al., 2011; Devi et al, 2013; Panwar et al, 2022; Bhardwaj et al, 2019; UNCCD, 2018).

The Lower Shivalik region of the Indian Himalayas represents a fragile agro-ecological zone characterized by undulating to steep slopes, shallow and coarse-textured soils, and high rainfall intensity concentrated during the monsoon season. These inherent biophysical constraints, coupled with increasing anthropogenic pressure, render the region highly vulnerable to land degradation. Agriculture in the Lower Shivaliks is predominantly rainfed and practiced on sloping lands, where conventional land use practices are poorly suited to the terrain, leading to inefficient resource use and ecosystem instability.

Severe soil erosion and excessive surface runoff are the most critical challenges affecting agricultural sustainability in the Lower Shivaliks. High runoff velocities on sloping lands result in substantial loss of fertile topsoil, nutrients, and soil organic carbon, ultimately degrading soil health and reducing crop productivity. Over time, declining yields, limited livelihood options, and increased production risks exacerbate socio-economic stress among farming communities. The degradation spiral further constrains household income, employment opportunities, and resilience to climatic variability.

Ecosystem services—defined as the benefits humans derive from ecosystems—provide a useful framework to evaluate the multifunctional outcomes of land management interventions. In sloping agro-ecosystems, technologies that integrate soil and water conservation with productive land use can simultaneously enhance provisioning and regulating ecosystem services. Sloping Agricultural Land Technology (SALT) is one such slope-adapted approach that combines agroforestry, contour-based cropping, hedgerows, and water harvesting to reduce erosion, regulate hydrological flows, improve soil fertility, and diversify production systems. Valuing these ecosystem services in economic terms helps capture the full benefits of such interventions beyond conventional yield metrics.

Although SALT and related conservation-based land use systems have been promoted for soil and water conservation in hilly regions, empirical evidence quantifying their ecosystem service benefits—particularly in monetary terms—remains limited. Most existing studies focus on biophysical outcomes such as runoff reduction or yield improvement, while integrated assessments linking ecological, hydrological, carbon, nutrient, and livelihood benefits under a unified ecosystem service framework are scarce for the Lower Shivalik region.

The study hypothesizes that slope-adapted land use interventions such as SALT significantly enhance regulating ecosystem services (soil conservation, runoff control, and carbon stock and water conservation/harvesting) while simultaneously improving provisioning services (crop, fodder, fuelwood, fish production, and employment generation) and supporting services (soil fertility) in hilly sub-tropical agro-ecosystems. The present study (1) investigates hydrological and soil health benefits as well as quantifies carbon sequestration and soil nutrient retention where SALT has been applied across slope positions and (2) evaluates livelihood benefits in terms of production, employment generation, and overall economic value of ecosystem services.

# Materials and methods
Study area
The study was carried out in the research farm of ICAR-Indian Institute of Soil and Water Conservation, Research Centre at Mansadevi, Panchkula, Haryana, India. The geographic coordinates of the study sites are 30°44′03.44″ N latitude and 76°51′16.37″ E longitude, and the elevation is 370 m above the mean sea level. The experiment was set up on a steep slope varying from 12% to 15%. As per the USDA soil taxonomy classification, the soil of the study area is a light-textured hyperthermic Udic Ustochrept (Grewal et al., 1996) that is sand to sandy loam in texture, well drained, and has a low water-holding capacity. The area receives 1,100 mm of mean annual rainfall, of which 80% occurs during the monsoon season (June to September). Farmers of the region practice a maize (rainy season) and wheat (winter season) cropping system.

Description of sloping agricultural land technology
At the initial stage of the experiment, the land was entirely covered by the obnoxious weed Lantana camara, and no revenue could be generated (Figure 1).
A 1-ha land was earmarked for developing the model that was divided into three parts: upper, middle, and lower. A silvi-horti-pasture agroforestry model was initiated at the upper part of the sloping land where 42 plants each of Emblica officinalis (aonla) and Melia composita (dake) were established at a spacing of 5 × 5m in alternative rows in 2010 and then intercropped with Eulaliopsis binata (bhabbar grass) in 2012–2013. Emblica officinalis is a fruit crop which grows well in dry regions and fetches a remunerative price in the market, while Melia composita is a fuelwood-cum-timber-yielding tree that grows well in the region. The wood of the species is used for making light furniture. Eulaliopsis binata is a hardy grass and good for soil and water conservation. In the middle portion, the sloping land was converted to terraces. On the outer edge of the terraces, 95 plants of Leuceana leucocephala (subabul) were established in a linear row (single line) on terrace risers at a spacing of 1 meterm in height. Leuceana is a leguminous species that fixes nitrogen in soil and can be used as fodder. Psidium guajava (guava) (57 plants), a fruit-yielding plant, was planted in the interspaces of Leucaena at a distance of 5 meterm within the row. The basic principle of agroforestry is to reduce competition (above- and belowground) among associated components. Reducing the height of the tree component automatically reduces aboveground competition as posed by shade. Thus, Leucaena and guava were maintained as hedges. The agricultural crops usually cultivated by farmers in this region, including Sesamum indicum (sesame), Eruca sativa (rocket), Zea mays (maize), Vigna mungo (black gram), and Vigna radiata (green gram), were grown on the terraces. Although the predominant cropping system of the region is maize–wheat, oil seed crops such as sesame, rocket, and pulses were grown in places where assured irrigation was not available. After a contour survey was undertaken, a pond of 0.12-ha-meterm capacity was dug at the lower end of the sloping land to collect the runoff water generated from the catchment area (Figure 2). The harvested water was utilized for life-saving irrigation of horticultural and agricultural crops grown in the catchment area of the pond.

Ecosystem service indicators
The ecosystem services provided by the sloping agricultural land system were quantified in 2022. Since the system had a plot scale expanse, only few provisioning, regulating, and supporting services were identified and quantified. Provisioning services obtained from the system were crop yield, fodder, fuelwood, timber, fish production, and employment generation, and regulating services included soil conservation, runoff control, and carbon stock and water conservation/harvesting, while soil fertility was a supporting service.

Quantifying ecosystem services
Crop yield was obtained by taking biomass data in 1 × 1-m quadrate with five replicates. The economic part of the crop was separated and weighed and converted to tonnes per hectare. Harvesting of Leucaena was done three times in a year (July, October, and December). All of the branches/leaves above a height of 1 m were removed to maintain it as a hedgerow, oven-dried, and weighed. The biomass and carbon estimation method for trees is given in the following section. Fish were harvested in November, directly weighed, and sold. To quantify the value of employment generated, we recorded the number of labor employed in each activity throughout the years and multiplied this by the average labor rate (Table 1) and number of man-days. Since the system was of 1 ha, total enumeration was done. Grass and crop biomass were quantified by taking representative samples in 1 × 1-m areas.

Ecosystem service components and measurements
Soil sampling
Soil samples were collected following standard procedures. Sampling locations were selected randomly as per the standard procedure of soil sampling. Five replicates were taken from each land use. At each replicate, soil samples were taken at three depths (0–15, 15–30, and 30–45 cm). The soil samples were air-dried, ground, and passed through a 2-mm sieve. Soil organic carbon (OC) was determined using the wet digestion method of Walkley and Black (1934).

Runoff and sediment loss
To measure runoff and soil loss, a gauging station was installed, which had an automatic stage level recorder that draws graphs based on the volume of flowing water passing through the weir. The weir installed was V notch with a height of 30 cm (Figure 3). Time vs. stage relationships (height of the flow of water) marked on the graphs were read, and the water discharged from the V notch was calculated with the help of empirical formulae. The runoff was monitored every monsoon from 2010 until 2015. Results
Provisioning services
Crop production, fodder production, grasses, fish production, and employment generation were the provisioning services obtained from the system. These services were categorized into two: production and employment services.

Production services obtained from the system and their contributions are given in Figure 4. The total crop production was 5.47 Mg ha-1, of which the highest production contribution was 3.47 Mg ha-1 (63%) obtained from grasses. These production services were monetized (Figure 5). Monetary contributions were highest for sesame (INR 7,621) and lowest for Leucaena (INR 805). Although the production contribution was highest for grasses, in terms of money, however, it contributed only 12.78%. Apart from crop and fodder production, the system was able to produce fish from the harvested water between July and mid-November. A total of 25 kg fish was sold for INR 2,500 (Table 2).
Employment services: The system is an integration of different components where manpower is continuously required for management. Figure 6 reflects the proportion of land dedicated to the different components of the system. Annually, 50 man-days were required to manage agriculture, 30 man-days for horticulture, and 20 man-days for forestry. Thus, a total 100 man-days are required per year to manage the system. Prior to the establishment of the new system, approximately 20 man-days were employed to control Lantana camara. In total, the system generated an additional employment of 80 man-days ha-1 year-1.
Regulating services
Soil conservation, hydrological improvement, biomass accumulation, and carbon sequestration were the regulating services provided by the system.

Soil conservation and hydrological benefits
Runoff and soil erosion
In the integrated system established, runoff was properly channelized through terraces to reduce the velocity of the water. The runoff was stored in the farm pond at the lowest slope point of the farm. As a result, 8,086 m3 ha-1 of water was collected as against 4,703 m3 ha-1 if these measures were not taken. The additional water harvested amounted to a saving of INR 23,681 ha-1 year-1. In addition, the system was able to reduce soil loss by 0.91 t ha-1 year-1 that amounted to a value of INR 1,171 ha-1 year-1, including expenses incurred for desilting the same amount of soil from the farm pond (Table 2).

Soil fertility loss estimation
Nutrients lost from the system were measured as nutrients lost through soil loss. The soil lost from the system was analyzed for nutrient content (N, P, and K). Nutrient loss was monetized by taking the cost of fertilizers required to replace the same amount from the system. The economic loss through soil loss was negligible (Table 2).

Biomass and CO2 sequestration
The silvi-horti-pasture agroforestry system was established in the upper one-third part of the system along with hedgerow intercropping on the terrace, resulting in 0.4 ha under tree cover. Before the establishment of the system, the land was infested by Lantana camara with a biomass of 17 Mg ha-1. After Lantana was uprooted and SALT was established, biomass accumulation increased to 141.45 Mg ha-1. Thus, as a result of incorporating perennial components, an additional 62.2 Mg ha-1 of carbon (taking 50% carbon in biomass) was sequestered (Table 2). The carbon sequestered provided an additional benefit of INR 56,874 ha-1 (taking the exchange rate in 2010 as INR 45.70 per US dollar).

Supporting services
The change in soil fertility was taken as a supporting service provided by the system. Soil nutrients (N, P, and K) were assessed before and after system establishment. We found that as a result of system establishment, the nutrient content in soil decreased when compared to the previous situation. The available nitrogen decreased from 288.2 to 195.3 kg ha-1 and the available potassium decreased from 265.09 to 255.4 kg ha-1, whereas there was no change in soil phosphorous content. To retain the fertility status at its original level, INR 656 ha-1 had to be incurred (Table 2).

# Discussion
Investigations on the ecosystem services generated from a 1-ha SALT model established on a 12%–15% slope integrating silvi-horti-pasture systems on upper slopes, terraced hedgerow-based agriculture on middle slopes, and runoff harvesting through a farm pond at the lower slope revealed that provisioning and regulating services were dominant. Provisioning services generated multiple livelihood benefits, including 5.47 Mg ha-¹ of crops, fodder, fuelwood, and grasses and 80 additional man-days ha-¹ year-¹ of employment. Regulating services increased the runoff by harvesting 3,383 m³ ha-¹ year-¹, reduced soil loss by 0.91 t ha-¹ year-¹, and sequestered 62.2 Mg ha-1 of additional carbon. The total monetized ecosystem service value increased from INR 51,837 ha-¹ under degraded conditions to INR 174,052 ha-¹ after SALT establishment.

Similar results have been obtained by other studies—for example, Raveloaritiana et al. (2023), who studied multiple land uses in northeastern Madagascar, found that, on average, 88.4% of the interviewed households per village reported benefiting from provisioning services from mosaic landscapes, while 51.9% ± 6.1% mentioned regulating services. Zhao et al. (2020), who assessed the ecosystem service value (ESV) of the Sanmenxia Reservoir wetland in China, confirmed that provisioning services comprised the largest share, followed by regulating services.

Provisioning ecosystem services and livelihood enhancement
The implementation of Sloping Agricultural Land Technology (SALT) transformed a completely degraded, Lantana camara-infested site into a productive agro-ecosystem delivering multiple provisioning services. The system generated a total biomass yield of 5.47 Mg ha-¹ comprising crops, fodder, fuelwood, and grasses (Table 2). Grasses alone contributed 3.47 Mg ha-¹ (63% of total biomass), demonstrating their suitability for sloping lands and dual role in fodder supply and soil protection. Despite their lower market value (12.8% of production value), grasses are critical for sustaining livestock-based livelihoods in rainfed hill systems. Conversely, crop contributions from sesame, maize, pulses, and oilseeds were disproportionate to monetary returns, with sesame providing the highest economic contribution (INR 7,621 ha-¹) despite a lower biomass share. This highlights the importance of integrating high-value crops within conservation-based systems to enhance farm profitability. In addition, harvesting 25 kg of fish from stored runoff water added INR 2,500 (Table 2), demonstrating how hydrological regulation under SALT directly translates into additional food and income sources. Leijster et al. (2021) also concluded that integrating agroforestry in coffee farms improved the supply of multiple ecosystem services over time and mostly followed an asymptotic relationship with periods of steep increase (0–10 years), little increase (11–20 years), and stabilization after 20 years. These results align with those of Kumar (2016), who concluded that smallholder agroforestry provides an array of diversified products and contributes to achieving food and nutritional security. Similarly, Keerthika et al. (2025), who valued provisioning services from multifunctional agroforestry in the Nilgiris in Tamil Nadu, India, reported values of $1,136 (INR 102,240) for timber and $7,831.45 (INR 704,830.5) for food, fodder, flower, fruits, medicinal plants, and tree-borne oil seeds (1 US $ = INR 90).

Employment generation increased markedly from 20 to 100 man-days ha-¹ year-¹, resulting in a net gain of 80 man-days ha-¹ year-¹. This fivefold increase reflects the labor demand associated with diversified system components—agriculture (50 man-days), horticulture (30 man-days), and forestry (20 man-days)—and highlights SALT’s role in addressing rural employment deficits in the Lower Shivalik areas (Table 2).

Regulating services increase water and soil conservation and carbon sequestration
Regulating ecosystem services improved substantially following SALT establishment. The annual runoff water harvested increased from 4,703 to 8,086 m³ ha-¹, yielding an additional 3,383 m³ ha-¹ of stored water. Monetized at prevailing water values, these gains resulted in economic savings of INR 23,681 ha-¹ year-¹ (Table 2). Furthermore, these results demonstrate SALT’s effectiveness in converting high runoff losses into productive water resources since 80% of the region’s 1,100-mm annual rainfall occurs during the monsoon. The increase in water-related ecosystem services consequent to establishing woody vegetation has had similar results in Australia (Chen and Vardon, 2024). However, Bagstad et al. (2019) showed that the converse is also true. In their work in Rwanda, ecosystem services diminished with decreased vegetation cover. Evapotranspiration was reduced, resulting in more water being available for runoff. Consequently, the national water yield increased from 7.14 billion m3 in 1990 to 7.42 billion m3 in 2015. Liu et al. (2024) studied the ecosystem service value (ESV) of Sanmenxia Reservoir wetland in China for two decades based on the analysis of land use patterns. They found that the ESV increased from 1.81 billion to 2.45 billion CNY in two decades, and the highest contributor was the water body (83.90%–88.71%), followed by croplands (7.06%–13.30%) and bare land.

Soil loss declined from 1.03 to 0.12 Mg ha-¹ year-¹, representing a reduction of 0.91 Mg ha-¹ year-¹. This reduction corresponds to avoided desilting costs valued at Rs. 1,171 ha-¹ year-¹ (Table 2). Similar results have been obtained by Portalanza et al. (2025) while working in El Cajas National Park, Ecuador. They observed that relative to a bare soil scenario, existing páramo grasslands, shrub mosaics, and scattered Polylepis spp. woodlots (high-altitude, mountainous woodlands found in the Andes) avert 95% of potential erosion, quantifying the service supplied by vegetation. Bagstad et al. (2019) studied the ecosystem accounts in Rwanda and found that the total amount of sediment exported across the country increased from 6.3 million t ha-1 in 1990 to 14.0 million t ha-1 in 2015. On average, sediment export rose from 2.5 to 5.5 tons ha-1 year-1 mainly due to the loss of vegetation cover. These results reflect the importance of ground cover in preventing soil erosion. Similar outputs were delivered by Leijster et al. (2021) in their study of Columbian coffee farms. They found that agroforestry systems had, on average, 48% lower potential soil losses than the monoculture coffee farms. Moreover, in Nicaragua, soils in coffee agroforests were covered with more litter, thus reducing soil erosion (Blanco et al., 2015). Tree cover characteristics in tree-based land use systems are widely reported to be related to ecosystem service supply (Klein et al., 2003), carbon stock (Jezeer et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2018), and erosion control (Cannavo et al., 2011). Such an erosion control ecosystem service is particularly significant in the Shivalik region, where shallow, sandy soils are highly vulnerable to degradation under conventional sloping agriculture.

In this study, a major regulating benefit of SALT was enhanced biomass accumulation and carbon sequestration. The replacement of Lantana camara (17 Mg ha-¹ biomass) with agroforestry-based systems further increased the total biomass to 141.45 Mg ha-¹. This resulted in an additional 62.2 Mg ha-¹ of carbon sequestered across the system, largely attributable to silvi-horti-pasture on upper slopes and hedgerow agroforestry on terraces. The monetized carbon benefits amounted to INR 56,874 ha-¹ (Table 2), accounting for nearly one-third of the total ecosystem service value. Adelisardou et al. (2023) studied land-use-change-related ecosystem services in Slovakia and found that carbon storage increased at a rate of 5.8%. This is one of the most vital ES that reduces atmospheric CO2 which accelerates climate change. The total amount of carbon storage in the research area was found to increase from 11,714,261 Mg C in 2000 to 12,397,747 Mg C in 2018 (at a rate of 5.8%). These results demonstrate the strong climate mitigation potential of slope-adapted agroforestry systems and their relevance for land degradation neutrality and carbon-market-linked incentive mechanisms.

Supporting services and soil fertility
Nutrient loss through runoff was minimal under SALT, indicating improved nutrient retention. However, available soil nitrogen declined from 288.2 to 195.3 kg ha-¹ and potassium from 265.1 to 255.4 kg ha-¹, while phosphorus remained unchanged. The decline in N and K likely reflects increased nutrient uptake by crops, grasses, and perennial tree components rather than enhanced losses. The estimated cost of restoring soil fertility to pre-treatment levels was modest (INR 656 ha-¹), suggesting that productivity gains under SALT can be sustained with targeted nutrient management or a better selection of species in the landscape design. Improved soil organic matter, although not directly monetized, represents a key supporting service that underpins long-term soil health, water retention, and biological activity. Reduced erosion combined with continuous organic inputs from grasses and tree litter likely contributed to this improvement.

Integrated ecosystem service valuation and sustainability implications
Overall, the total monetized ecosystem service value increased from INR 51,837 ha-¹ under degraded conditions to INR 174,052 ha-¹ (Table 2) following SALT implementation, yielding a net gain of INR 124,558 ha-¹. Regulating services—particularly water conservation and carbon sequestration—contributed the largest share of total value, emphasizing benefits that are often overlooked in conventional agricultural assessments.

These findings confirm that SALT delivers synergistic gains across provisioning and regulating services, making it a resilient and economically viable land use strategy for semi-arid hilly regions. The clear quantification of ecosystem service benefits strengthens the case for adopting SALT in watershed development program and for integrating ecosystem service valuation into policy instruments such as payments for ecosystem services and climate-smart agriculture initiatives.

Conclusion
Slope-adapted deployment of Sloping Agricultural Land Technology (SALT) substantially enhanced ecosystem service delivery in the Lower Shivalik region. The total monetized ecosystem service value increased from INR 51,837 ha-¹ under degraded conditions to INR 174,052 ha-¹, yielding a net gain of INR 124,558 ha-¹. Provisioning services improved with 5.47 Mg ha-¹ of biomass production and 80 additional man-days ha-¹ year-¹ of employment. Regulating services showed marked gains, with runoff harvesting increasing by 3,383 m³ ha-¹ year-¹, soil loss reduction by 0.91 Mg ha-¹ year-¹, and 62.2 Mg ha-¹ of additional carbon being sequestered, all of which contributed to INR 56,874 ha-¹ in climate mitigation value. These quantitative benefits demonstrate that SALT enhances resilience, resource-use efficiency, and livelihood security. It has strong potential for adoption in watershed development programs and for integration into carbon credit and payment-for-ecosystem-services mechanisms.
